THE EUROPEAN COMMISSION,

Having regard to the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union,

Having regard to Regulation (EU) 2016/1036 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 8 June 2016 on protection against dumped imports from countries not members of the European Union [^ntr1-L_202602512EN.000101-E0001] (‘the basic Regulation’), and in particular Article 7 thereof,

After consulting the Member States,

Whereas:

### 1. **PROCEDURE**

#### 1.1.   **Initiation**

- (1) On 12 March 2026, the European Commission (‘the Commission’) initiated an anti-dumping investigation into imports of certain acrylic esters originating in the People’s Republic of China (‘China’ or ‘the PRC’), the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (‘Saudi Arabia’), the Republic of South Africa (‘South Africa’) and the United States of America (‘the USA’), known together as ‘the countries concerned’ pursuant to Article 5 of the basic Regulation.

- (2) This followed a complaint lodged on 28 January 2026 by the Consortium for certain acrylic esters (‘the complainant’ or ‘the complainants’) on behalf of the Union industry within the meaning of Article 5(4) of the basic Regulation.

- (3) The initiation was justified by the evidence of both dumping and resulting material injury provided in the complaint, as set out in the Notice of Initiation in the *Official Journal of the European Union* [^ntr2-L_202602512EN.000101-E0002] (‘the Notice of Initiation’).

#### 1.2.   **Registration**

- (4) The Commission made imports of the product concerned subject to registration by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/1085 [^ntr3-L_202602512EN.000101-E0003] (‘the registration Regulation’).

#### 1.3.   **Interested parties**

- (5) In the Notice of Initiation, the Commission invited interested parties to participate in the investigation. Specifically, it notified the complainant, known Union producers, exporting producers, the authorities of the countries concerned and other known stakeholders - including importers, suppliers, users, and trade associations. All interested parties were given the opportunity to comment on the initiation and to request a hearing with the Commission or the Hearing Officer in trade proceedings.

- (6) On 18 March 2026 Arkema Inc (a producer in the United States of America) made a request to be an interested party. This request was not accepted as the company did not export the product concerned to the Union during the investigation period.

- (7) On 19 March 2026 Taixing Sunke Chemicals Co., Ltd. (a producer in China) made a request to be an interested party. Again, this request was not accepted as the company did not export the product concerned to the Union during the investigation period.

- (8) On 25 March 2026 and 3 April 2026, Arkema Inc. and Taixing Sunke Chemicals Co. Ltd requested the intervention of the Hearing Officer regarding the Commission’s decision to reject their requests to be interested parties, which was because both companies did not export the product concerned to the Union during the investigation period. Both entities are related to the Union producer Arkema France.

- (9) The Hearing Officer suggested to both companies that they should provide evidence of all the elements needed to show that the companies were genuinely affected by the outcome of the case. The companies did provide further evidence. However, the Commission decided that such elements were not considered relevant and the request was therefore denied.

- (10) Rather, on 15 July 2026 the services replied to both companies noting that as part of the Arkema Group both companies could make comments in this investigation and access the file via Arkema France.

- (11) The Hearing Officer concluded that it appeared a matter of internal coordination within the Arkema Group how to best participate in the investigation and protect its procedural rights accordingly.

#### 1.4.   **Comments on initiation**

- (12) The Saudi General Authority for Foreign Trade (‘GAFT’), Sasol South Africa Limited (‘Sasol’) and Organik Kimya Netherlands BV (‘Organik’) submitted that the level of confidentiality applied to the complaint was excessive, preventing them from exercising their right of defence and from understanding the claims raised in the complaint.

- (13) Sasol argued specifically that there was an absence of good cause for confidentiality and that the macro- and micro-injury indicators provided in ranges or indexed form comprised aggregated data that did not specifically target any individual Union producer and therefore should have been disclosed.

- (14) GAFT further submitted that the complaint failed to disclose important information, in particular with respect to normal value, export price, injury analysis and even the names of the complainants.

- (15) First, the Commission found that the information provided in the open version of the complaint permitted a reasonable understanding of the substance of the information submitted in line with Article 19(2) of the basic Regulation, including on issues such as normal value, export price and injury analysis.

- (16) Second, regarding the confidential treatment of the complainants’ identity, it was noted that the disclosure of their names would have a significantly adverse effect on them, given the risks of retaliation. In any event, non-disclosure of the company names behind the complaint did not affect the ability of interested parties to comment meaningfully on the substance of the complaint.

- (17) Third, the Commission accepted aggregated ranges and indexed data for the micro-economic and macro-economic indicators in view of the limited number of Union producers. Nevertheless, the ranges and indexes provided sufficient detail to permit an assessment of the trends and developments of all the injury indicators, thereby ensuring that the rights of defence of interested parties are fully respected.

- (18) The fact that in the present case, GAFT, Sasol as well as Organik were able to submit detailed observations, including on the information provided in a non-confidential, indexed or aggregated form, further demonstrated that the parties were able to comment effectively on the complaint. The arguments of the parties were therefore rejected.

- (19) Furthermore, the Chinese exporter Pinghu Petro Chemical Co., Ltd (‘Pinghu Petro’) claimed that the complainant did not sufficiently demonstrate that it was representative of the Union industry in accordance with Article 5(4) of the basic Regulation. The party argued that the complainant provided no evidence that the 50 % threshold laid down in Article 5(4) of the basic Regulation has been met.

- (20) As part of the statutory analysis of the complaint, the Commission services have carried out a standing examination before initiation. For this purpose, all Union producers known to the Commission have been contacted before initiation and taken into consideration in the calculation of the degree of support. The outcome of the standing exercise showed that the complaint was lodged by or on behalf of the Union industry in line with Article 5(4) of the basic Regulation and that the relevant thresholds set out in the said Article have been met.

- (21) The 50 % threshold disputed by Pinghu Petro is only assessed after the complaint has been lodged and immediately prior to the initiation. Consequently, and contrary to Pinghu Petro’s claim, the complainant was not in the position to assess reliably the level of support for the complaint, nor was it legally required to do so. It was further recalled that a note for the file on standing was included in the open file and confirmed that standing has been established before initiation, as the replies to the standing exercise provided by the individual Union producers (despite being provided in ranges) indicated that also the 50 % threshold prescribed by Article 5(4) of the basic Regulation was met. Pinghu Petro’s claim was therefore dismissed.

- (22) According to GAFT, the Commission violated Article 5.3 of the WTO Anti-Dumping Agreement (‘ADA’) in that it initiated the investigation on the basis of an incomplete and legally deficient complaint, which omitted several mandatory injury factors listed in Article 3.4 of ADA from its analysis (e.g. productivity, cash flow, wages, growth).

- (23) First, the legal standard of evidence required for a complaint under Article 5 of the basic Regulation (corresponding to Article 5 of ADA) makes it clear that the quantity and quality of information in the complaint is not the same as the one on which the Commission bases its findings during an investigation. In fact, the complaint needs to include sufficient evidence of dumping, injury and a causal link which is reasonably available to the complainant (which was the case here).

- (24) Further to that, Article 5 of the basic Regulation does not require that all injury factors mentioned in Article 3(5) (equivalent to Article 3.4 of ADA) are analysed or show deterioration to establish evidence of material injury sufficient to initiate an investigation.

- (25) Indeed, the wording of Article 5(2) of the basic Regulation states that the complaint must contain information on changes in the volume of the allegedly dumped imports, the effect of those imports on prices of the like product on the Union market and the consequent impact of the imports on the Union industry, as demonstrated by relevant (but not necessarily all) factors and indices having a bearing on the state of the Union industry, such as those listed in Articles 3(3) and 3(5) of the basic Regulation. Therefore, GAFT’s claim was dismissed.

#### 1.5.   **Sampling**

- (26) In the Notice of Initiation, the Commission stated that it might sample interested parties in accordance with Article 17 of the basic Regulation.

*Sampling of Union producers*

- (27) In the Notice of Initiation, the Commission informed parties that it had provisionally selected a sample of three Union producers based on production and sales quantities. The sampled Union producers accounted for over 80% of the total Union production.

- (28) The Commission invited interested parties to comment on the provisional sample. No comments were received and the definitive sample was found to be representative of the Union industry.

*Sampling of unrelated importers*

- (29) To determine if sampling was necessary, the Commission asked unrelated importers to provide the information specified in the Notice of Initiation.

- (30) As only two importers provided the requested information, the Commission decided that sampling was not necessary.

*Sampling of exporting producers*

- (31) To determine if sampling was necessary, the Commission asked all exporting producers in the countries concerned to provide the information specified in the Notice of Initiation. In addition, the Commission asked the Missions of the countries concerned to identify and/or contact other exporting producers that could be interested in participating in the investigation.

- (32) The Commission received five replies from Chinese exporting producers, one reply from Saudi Arabia, one reply from South Africa and two replies from exporting producers in the USA. They provided the requested information and agreed to be included in the sample. One producer from the USA did not export the product concerned during the investigation period and its sampling reply was therefore disregarded.

- (33) In view of the low number of replies from Saudi Arabia, South Africa and the USA, the Commission decided that sampling was not necessary. Consequently, all exporting producers that came forward from these countries were invited to submit a questionnaire reply.

- (34) Given the number of replies from Chinese exporting producers, and in accordance with Article 17(1) of the basic Regulation, the Commission selected a sample of two exporting producers.

- (35) This selection was based on the largest representative volume of exports to the Union that could reasonably be investigated within the time available. In accordance with Article 17(2) of the basic Regulation, the Commission consulted all known exporting producers and the authorities of China on the selection. The Commission did not receive any comments on the sample.

#### 1.6.   **Questionnaire replies and verification visits**

- (36) The Commission sent a questionnaire concerning the existence in China of significant distortions within the meaning of Article 2(6a)(b) of the basic Regulation to the Government of the People’s Republic of China (‘GOC’).

- (37) The Commission sent questionnaires to the sampled Union producers, sampled exporting producers in China, known exporting producers in Saudi Arabia, USA and South Africa, known importers and users. On the date of initiation, the Commission made the same questionnaires available online [^ntr4-L_202602512EN.000101-E0004]. After the deadline to return the completed questionnaire, the Saudi exporting producer, SAMCO, informed the Commission that it would not be submitting a reply and provided reasons for its non-cooperation. On 27 April 2026, the Commission sent a letter to SAMCO informing them of its intention to apply Article 18 of the basic Regulation. No reply was received.

- (38) The Commission sought and verified all the information deemed necessary for a provisional determination of dumping, resulting injury and Union interest. Verification visits pursuant to Article 16 of the basic Regulation were carried out at the premises of the following companies:

Union producers:

- — Synthomer a.s., Sokolov, Czech Republic (‘Synthomer’);

- — BASF SE, Ludwigshafen, Germany (‘BASF’);

- — Arkema France SA, Carling, France (‘Arkema’);

Exporting producer in USA:

- — Rohm and Haas Texas Inc, Midland and Deer Park, USA, Rohm and Haas Chemical LLC, Midland, USA, and Union Carbide Corporation, Midland and New Orleans, USA (‘Dow US’);

Exporting producers in China:

- — Pinghu Petro Chemical Co., Ltd, Pinghu, China (‘Pinghu Petro’);

- — Shanghai Huayi New Material Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China (‘Huayi’);

Exporting producer in South Africa:

- — Sasol South Africa Limited, Johannesburg, South Africa (‘Sasol’);

Related importers:

- — Dow Europe GmbH, Switzerland (Dow Europe);

- — Sasol Chemie Hamburg, Germany.

#### 1.7.   **Investigation period and period considered**

- (39) The investigation of dumping and injury covered the period from 1 January 2025 to 31 December 2025 (‘the investigation period’ or ‘IP’). The examination of trends relevant for the assessment of injury covered the period from 1 January 2022 to the end of the investigation period (‘the period considered’).

### 2. **PRODUCT UNDER INVESTIGATION, PRODUCT CONCERNED AND LIKE PRODUCT**

#### 2.1.   **Product under investigation**

- (40) The ‘product under investigation’ is certain acrylic esters (‘AE’) which are butyl acrylate (‘BA’) and 2-ethylhexyl acrylate (‘2EHA’). BA is currently falling under Chemical Abstracts Service number (‘CAS RN’) 141-32-2 and Customs Union and Statistics Code (‘CUS’) 0012413-0, and 2EHA is currently falling under CAS RN 103-11-7 and CUS 0017228-1.

- (41) The product scope excludes other essential acrylates like Ethyl Acrylate (EA) or Methyl Acrylate (MA) and specialty acrylates like Alkyl acrylates, Hydroxy-alkyl acrylates or Dialkylaminoethylacrylates.

- (42) BA and 2EHA are considered as one homogenous single product as they are both esters made from the same raw material (in majority propylene) and their production methods and areas of application are almost identical. Propylene is the main raw material used in the production of acrylic esters (accounting for around 50 % of the total production costs).

- (43) Acrylic esters impart colour stability and clarity, heat and ageing resistance, weather resistance, low temperature flexibility and/or other qualities to the polymeric materials in which they are used.

- (44) The main end uses of these polymers include the production of coatings, adhesives and sealants, paper, polymer additives, textiles, and inks. BA’s applications are larger than those of 2EHA. However, they both remain present on the same market of soft monomers which differ in terms of technical properties and applications from those of MA and EA.

- (45) BA and 2EHA are substitutable in many applications but cannot be substituted with MA/EA because of the different technical properties and uses.

#### 2.2.   **Product concerned**

- (46) The ‘product concerned’ is the product under investigation originating in the People’s Republic of China, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, the Republic of South Africa, and the United States of America (‘the countries concerned’), currently classified under CN code ex 2916 12 00 (TARIC code 2916 12 00 15).

#### 2.3.   **Like product**

- (47) The investigation showed that the following products have the same basic physical chemical and technical characteristics as well as the same basic uses:

- — the product concerned when exported to the Union;

- — the product under investigation produced and sold on the domestic market of countries concerned; and

- — the product under investigation produced and sold in the Union by the Union industry.

- (48) The Commission decided at this stage that those products are therefore like products within the meaning of Article 1(4) of the basic Regulation.

#### 2.4.   **Claims regarding product scope**

- (49) Sasol claimed that BA and 2EHA differ substantively in terms of physical characteristics, prices and end uses. Therefore, according to this exporter, the injury analysis must be conducted on a segmented basis. In Sasol’s view, the segmented analysis should extend beyond price effects to encompass the assessment of volume effects, economic indicators, and the causal link or otherwise segment-specific trends may be masked.

- (50) The Commission disagreed with Sasol’s assessment. BA and 2EHA are acrylic esters, which are both substitutable on a functional and market basis, even if they are not strictly identical molecules. Both esters are produced in a condensation reaction (known as esterification) between crude acrylic acid and an alcohol. Moreover, from a chemical standpoint, BA and 2EHA belong to the same family of ‘soft’ acrylic esters.

- (51) They have very similar roles in coatings formulations: lowering the glass transition temperature (Tg), imparting flexibility, improving film formation, and contributing to adhesion. In practical formulation work, partial or total substitution between BA and 2EHA is feasible and commonly considered when optimizing system performance.

- (52) This reinforces the conclusion that the two esters compete within the same functional space and can substitute for each other under many circumstances. In this they are no different from product types of more homogeneous product.

- (53) Furthermore, from an industrial and market perspective, BA and 2EHA fit within the same value chain and are supplied to largely overlapping customer bases. Both are derived from similar upstream raw materials (notably propylene via oxo-alcohol processes), and both are used in comparable downstream applications. They are often grouped together as ‘heavy acrylate esters’ and distinguished collectively from lighter, more polar monomers such as ethyl acrylate (EA) or methyl acrylate (MA). This common positioning supports the view that they belong to the same product and market segment.

- (54) Moreover, it was recalled that the Commission enjoys a broad discretion in determination of injury caused to the Union industry. The basic Regulation does not, in principle, impose any obligation on the Commission to carry out an injury analysis and in particular the existence of price undercutting at a level other than that of the like product [^ntr5-L_202602512EN.000101-E0005]. Provided that the relevant differences between product types were taken into consideration in the PCN, the Court stated [^ntr6-L_202602512EN.000101-E0006] that the application of the PCN method, would normally take account of the market segments of the product under consideration for the purposes, in particular, of the analysis of price undercutting. Indeed, while the injury and causality analysis in this case is conducted based on aggregated, AE-related (covering both BA and 2EHA) information on imports, production and sales, dumping as well as injury calculations are performed on a product type basis to recognize price variations between BA and 2EHA and reflect price differences across these product types in the final determination of the dumping and injury margins.

- (55) In conclusion, in exercising its broad discretion and given that both BA and 2EHA (i) belong to the same product category of heavy acrylate esters, (ii) operate within the same market segment serving largely overlapping customer bases, and (iii) in light of the fact that both dumping as well as injury (undercutting/underselling) calculations are performed separately for BA and 2EHA, the Commission determined that no segmented assessment of volume effects, economic indicators or the causal link is required in this case. The Commission therefore rejected Sasol’s claim.

##### 3.2.2.   *Normal value*

- (105) According to Article 2(1) of the basic Regulation, ‘the normal value shall normally be based on the prices paid or payable, in the ordinary course of trade, by independent customers in the exporting country’.

- (106) However, according to Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation, ‘in case it is determined … that it is not appropriate to use domestic prices and costs in the exporting country due to the existence in that country of significant distortions within the meaning of point (b), the normal value shall be constructed exclusively on the basis of costs of production and sale reflecting undistorted prices or benchmarks’, and ‘shall include an undistorted and reasonable amount of administrative, selling and general costs and for profits’ (‘administrative, selling and general costs’ is referred to as ‘SG & A costs’).

- (107) As further explained below, the Commission concluded in the present investigation that, based on the evidence available, the application of Article 2(6a) of the basic Regulation was appropriate.

##### 3.2.3.   *Existence of significant distortions*

- (108) Article 2(6a)(b) of the basic Regulation states that ‘significant distortions are those distortions which occur when reported prices or costs, including the costs of raw materials and energy, are not the result of free market forces as they are affected by substantial government intervention. In assessing the existence of significant distortions regard shall be had, inter alia, to the potential impact of one or more of the following elements:

- — the market in question being served to a significant extent by enterprises which operate under the ownership, control or policy supervision or guidance of the authorities of the exporting country;

- — state presence in firms allowing the state to interfere with respect to prices or costs;

- — public policies or measures discriminating in favour of domestic suppliers or otherwise influencing free market forces;

- — the lack, discriminatory application or inadequate enforcement of bankruptcy, corporate or property laws;

- — wage costs being distorted;

- — access to finance granted by institutions which implement public policy objectives or otherwise not acting independently of the state.’

- (109) As the list in Article 2(6a)(b) of the basic Regulation is non-cumulative, not all the elements need to be given for a finding of significant distortions. Moreover, the same factual circumstances may be used to demonstrate the existence of one or more of the elements of the list.

- (110) However, any conclusion on significant distortions within the meaning of Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation must be made on the basis of all the evidence at hand. The overall assessment on the existence of distortions may also take into account the general context and situation in the exporting country, in particular where the fundamental elements of the exporting country’s economic and administrative set-up provide the government with substantial powers to intervene in the economy in such a way that prices and costs are not the result of the free development of market forces.

- (111) Article 2(6a)(c) of the basic Regulation provides that ‘[w]here the Commission has well-founded indications of the possible existence of significant distortions as referred to in point (b) in a certain country or a certain sector in that country, and where appropriate for the effective application of this Regulation, the Commission shall produce, make public and regularly update a report describing the market circumstances referred to in point (b) in that country or sector’.

- (112) Pursuant to this provision, the Commission issued a country report concerning China [^ntr32-L_202602512EN.000101-E0032] (‘the Report’), which contains evidence of the existence of substantial government intervention at many levels of the economy, including specific distortions in many key factors of production (such as land, energy, capital, raw materials and labour) as well as selected sectors (such as chemical). Interested parties were invited to rebut, comment or supplement the evidence contained in the investigation file at the time of initiation. The Report concerning China was placed in the investigation file at the initiation stage. The complaint also contained some relevant evidence complementing the Report.

- (113) The complainant relied on the evidence contained in the Report to argue that there are distortions in the Chinese acrylic esters industry. These distortions stem from the organization of the People’s Republic of China (‘PRC’), which is based on the concept of social market economy that is developed under the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party (‘CCP’) and covers all essential aspects of the State.

- (114) According to the complaint, at the economic level, the CCP exercises a particularly tight control, which is translated into the high importance of the State-Owned Enterprises (‘SOE’) in the economy and the CCP’s strong leverage over the private sector. The complainant pointed to three main channels of intervention by the GOC in the Chinese economy: administrative, financial and regulatory control of the State [^ntr33-L_202602512EN.000101-E0033].

- (115) The complaint argued that in China, business entities operating under state ownership, control, policy supervision or governmental guidance constitute a fundamental component of the economic structure and that this reality extends to the Chinese chemical industry. In the sector of the product concerned, the degree of state ownership remains significant, with major acrylic esters producers being fully or partially state-owned, such as: Shanghai Huayi Group Corporation Limited (Huayi Group), Shenyang Chemical Co. and Wanhua Chemical Group Co., Ltd [^ntr34-L_202602512EN.000101-E0034].

- (116) Concerning the State influence in the acrylic esters sector, the complainant mentioned that the State presence in firms allows it to interfere with respect to prices or costs and provided as an example the intervention in the cost of electricity [^ntr35-L_202602512EN.000101-E0035].

In this regard, the complaint mentioned the 2024 China Report, referring for instance to the 14th FYP of Gansu Province, the Gansu 14th Raw Material Industry Development Plan (‘Gansu Plan’) which outlined its scope including petrochemical, steel, non-ferrous metals, building materials and other industries [^ntr36-L_202602512EN.000101-E0036]. The complaint mentioned similar plans in other places like Lianyungang and Taizhou [^ntr37-L_202602512EN.000101-E0037].

- (118) The complaint also mentioned other distortions affecting the acrylic esters market such as public policies discriminating in favour of domestic suppliers [^ntr38-L_202602512EN.000101-E0038] and inadequate enforcement of bankruptcy, corporate or property laws [^ntr39-L_202602512EN.000101-E0039]. The complaint also explained that the intervention of the State in the economy also has an impact on the wage costs [^ntr40-L_202602512EN.000101-E0040].

- (119) Moreover, the complainant explained that distortions also exist in the financial system, as access to finance is granted by institutions which implement public policy objectives or otherwise not acting independently of the State [^ntr41-L_202602512EN.000101-E0041].

- (120) In conclusion, the complainant argued that significant distortions pursuant to Article 2(6a) of the basic Regulation are present in the acrylic esters sector.

- (121) The Commission examined whether it was appropriate or not to use domestic prices and costs in China, due to the existence of significant distortions within the meaning of point (b) of Article 2(6a) of the basic Regulation. The Commission did so on the basis of the evidence available on the file. The evidence on the file included the evidence contained in the Report, which relies on publicly available sources.

- (122) That analysis covered the examination of the substantial government interventions in China’s economy in general, but also the specific market situation in the relevant sector including the product concerned. The Commission further supplemented these evidentiary elements with its own research on the various criteria relevant to confirm the existence of significant distortions in China.

###### 3.2.3.1.   Significant distortions affecting the domestic prices and costs in China

- (123) The Chinese economic system is based on the concept of a ‘socialist market economy’. That concept is enshrined in the Chinese Constitution and determines the economic governance of China. The core principle is the ‘socialist public ownership of the means of production, namely, ownership by the whole people and collective ownership by the working people’ [^ntr42-L_202602512EN.000101-E0042].

- (124) The state-owned economy is the ‘leading force in the national economy’ and the state has the mandate to ensure its ‘consolidation and growth’ [^ntr43-L_202602512EN.000101-E0043]. Indeed, compared to the 13th FYP, SASAC confirmed that total assets of central enterprises grew by 44,6 % during the 14th FYP, ‘effectively driving the integrated development of upstream and downstream enterprises in the industrial chain and providing strong support for the successful achievement of the main goals and tasks of my country’s economic and social development’ [^ntr44-L_202602512EN.000101-E0044].

- (125) Consequently, the overall setup of the Chinese economy not only allows for substantial government interventions into the economy, but such interventions are expressly mandated. The notion of supremacy of public ownership over the private one permeates the entire legal system and is emphasized as a general principle in all central pieces of legislation.

- (126) The Chinese property law is a prime example: it refers to the primary stage of socialism and entrusts the state with upholding the basic economic system under which the public ownership plays a dominant role. Other forms of ownership are tolerated, with the law permitting them to develop side by side with the state ownership [^ntr45-L_202602512EN.000101-E0045].

- (127) In addition, under Chinese law, the socialist market economy is developed under the leadership of the CCP. The structures of the Chinese state and of the CCP are intertwined at every level (legal, institutional, personal), forming a superstructure in which the roles of CCP and the state are indistinguishable.

- (128) Following an amendment of the Chinese Constitution in March 2018, the leading role of the CCP was given an even greater prominence by being reaffirmed in the text of Article 1 of the Constitution.

- (129) Following the already existing first sentence of the provision: ‘[t]he socialist system is the basic system of the People’s Republic of China’ a new second sentence was inserted which reads: ‘[t]he defining feature of socialism with Chinese characteristics is the leadership of the Communist Party of China’ [^ntr46-L_202602512EN.000101-E0046]. This illustrates the unquestioned and ever-growing control of the CCP over the economic system of China.

- (130) The leadership and control are inherent to the Chinese system and goes well beyond the situation customary in other countries where the governments exercise general macroeconomic control within the boundaries of which free market forces are at play.

- (131) The Chinese state engages in an interventionist economic policy in pursuance of goals, which coincide with the political agenda set by the CCP rather than reflecting the prevailing economic conditions in a free market [^ntr47-L_202602512EN.000101-E0047]. The interventionist economic tools deployed by the Chinese authorities are manifold, including the system of industrial planning, the financial system, as well as the level of the regulatory environment.

- (132) First, on the level of overall administrative control, the direction of the Chinese economy is governed by a complex system of industrial planning which affects all economic activities within the country. The totality of these plans covers a comprehensive and complex matrix of sectors and crosscutting policies and is present on all levels of government.

- (133) Plans at provincial level are detailed while national plans set broader targets. Plans also specify the means in order to support the relevant industries/sectors as well as the timeframes in which the objectives need to be achieved. Some plans still contain explicit output targets.

- (134) Under the plans, individual industrial sectors and/or projects are being singled out as (positive or negative) priorities in line with the government priorities and specific development goals are attributed to them (industrial upgrade, international expansion etc.).

- (135) The economic operators, private and state-owned alike, must effectively adjust their business activities according to the realities imposed by the planning system. This is not only because of the binding nature of the plans, but also because the relevant Chinese authorities at all levels of government adhere to the system of plans and use their vested powers accordingly, thereby inducing the economic operators to comply with the priorities set out in the plans [^ntr48-L_202602512EN.000101-E0048].

- (136) Second, on the level of allocation of financial resources, the financial system of China is dominated by the state-owned commercial and policy banks. Those banks, when setting up and implementing their lending policy need to align themselves with the government’s industrial policy objectives rather than primarily assessing the economic merits of a given project [^ntr49-L_202602512EN.000101-E0049].

- (137) The same applies to the other components of the Chinese financial system, such as the stock markets, bond markets, private equity markets etc. Also, these parts of the financial sector are institutionally and operationally set up in a manner not geared towards maximizing the efficient functioning of the financial markets but towards ensuring control and allowing intervention by the state and the CCP [^ntr50-L_202602512EN.000101-E0050].

- (138) Third, on the level of regulatory environment, the interventions by the state into the economy take a number of forms. For instance, the public procurement rules are regularly used in pursuit of policy goals other than economic efficiency, thereby undermining market-based principles in the area. The applicable legislation specifically provides that public procurement shall be conducted in order to facilitate the achievement of goals designed by state policies. However, the nature of these goals remains undefined, thereby leaving broad margin of appreciation to the decision-making bodies [^ntr51-L_202602512EN.000101-E0051].

- (139) Similarly, in the area of investment, the GOC maintains significant control and influence over destination and magnitude of both state and private investment. Investment screening as well as various incentives, restrictions, and prohibitions related to investment are used by authorities as an important tool for supporting industrial policy goals, such as maintaining state control over key sectors or bolstering domestic industry [^ntr52-L_202602512EN.000101-E0052].

- (140) In sum, the Chinese economic model is based on certain basic axioms, which provide for and encourage manifold government interventions. Such substantial government interventions are at odds with the free play of market forces, resulting in distorting the effective allocation of resources in line with market principles [^ntr53-L_202602512EN.000101-E0053].

- (141) In China, enterprises operating under the ownership, control and/or policy supervision or guidance by the state represent an essential part of the economy.

- (142) The sector of the product concerned is served by both private companies such as Satellite Chemicals Co. Ltd [^ntr54-L_202602512EN.000101-E0054]. and by state-owned companies such as Shanghai Huayi New Material Co. Other producers of acrylic esters [^ntr58-L_202602512EN.000101-E0058] are Wanhua Chemical Group Co. ###### 3.2.3.3.   Significant distortions according to Article 2(6a)(b), second indent of the basic Regulation: State presence in firms allowing the state to interfere with respect to prices or costs

- (157) The GOC is in position to interfere with prices and costs through state presence in firms. Indeed, CCP cells in enterprises, state-owned and private alike, represent an important channel through which the state can interfere with business decisions.

- (158) According to China’s company law, a CCP organisation is to be established in every company (with at least three CCP members as specified in the CCP Constitution [^ntr87-L_202602512EN.000101-E0087]) and the company shall provide the necessary conditions for the activities of the Party organisation.

- (159) In the past, this requirement appeared not to have always been followed or strictly enforced. However, since at least 2016 the CCP has been reinforcing its claims to control business decisions in companies as a matter of political principle [^ntr88-L_202602512EN.000101-E0088], including exercising pressure on private companies to put ‘patriotism’ first and to follow Party discipline [^ntr89-L_202602512EN.000101-E0089].

- (160) Already in 2017, it was reported that party cells existed in 70 % of some 1,86 million privately owned companies, with growing pressure for the CCP organisations to have a final say over the business decisions within their respective companies [^ntr90-L_202602512EN.000101-E0090]. These rules are of general application throughout the Chinese economy, across all sectors, including to the producers of the product concerned and the suppliers of their inputs.

- (161) In addition, on 15 September 2020 a document titled General Office of CCP Central Committee’s Guidelines on stepping up the United Front work in the private sector for the new era [^ntr91-L_202602512EN.000101-E0091] (‘the Guidelines’) was released, which further expanded the role of the Party committees in private enterprises.

The Guidelines thus emphasise and seek to increase the role of the CCP in companies and other private sector entities [^ntr92-L_202602512EN.000101-E0092].

###### 3.2.3.4.   Significant distortions according to Article 2(6a)(b), third indent of the basic Regulation: public policies or measures discriminating in favour of domestic suppliers or otherwise influencing free market forces

- (170) The direction of the Chinese economy is to a significant degree determined by an elaborate system of planning which sets out priorities and prescribes the goals the central, provincial and local governments must focus on. Relevant plans exist at all levels of government and cover virtually all economic sectors. The objectives set by the planning instruments are of a binding nature and the authorities at each administrative level monitor the implementation of the plans by the corresponding lower level of government.

- (171) Overall, the system of planning in China results in resources being driven to sectors designated as strategic or otherwise politically important by the government, rather than being allocated in line with market forces [^ntr104-L_202602512EN.000101-E0104].

- (172) The Chinese authorities have enacted a number of policies guiding the functioning of the sector of the product concerned.

- (173) The 14th FYP on economic and social development and 2035 perspectives [^ntr105-L_202602512EN.000101-E0105] aims to ‘upgrade traditional industries, promote the optimization and structural adjustment of raw material industries such as petrochemicals, steel, nonferrous metals, and building materials, expand the supply of high-quality products in sectors such as light industry and textiles, speed up the transformation and upgrading of enterprises in key industries such as the chemical industry and papermaking, and improve the green manufacturing system’ [^ntr106-L_202602512EN.000101-E0106].

- (174) According to the 14th FYP on the raw materials industry [^ntr107-L_202602512EN.000101-E0107], China ‘will develop a batch of industrial clusters in petrochemicals. […] In sectors including petrochemicals and chemicals, steel, non-ferrous metals, and building materials, [China] will foster a number of pioneering enterprises that could lead the ecosystem of the industrial chain with core competitiveness’ [^ntr108-L_202602512EN.000101-E0108].

- (175) Additionally, the Guiding Opinion on promoting the high quality development of the petrochemical and chemical industry [^ntr109-L_202602512EN.000101-E0109] requires to ‘strengthen sectoral policies and scientifically regulate the scale of the industry: […] enhance the supply capacity of high-end polymers, specialty chemicals and other products […]’ as well as to ‘[i]mprove supporting policies: strengthen the coordination of fiscal, financial, regional, investment, import and export, energy, ecological, environmental, price and other policies with industrial policies [and to g]ive full play to the role of the national industry-finance cooperation platform […]’.

- (176) At the provincial level, the Zhejiang 14th FYP on developing the Petrochemical and Chemical Industry [^ntr110-L_202602512EN.000101-E0110] mandates to ‘strengthen the guarantee of essential resources such as industrial capital, talent, land, and energy with a particular focus on leading petrochemical enterprises, major projects, and infrastructure development [as well as to] [o]ptimize and coordinate relevant provincial special funds to increase support for the development of the petrochemical industry’.

- (177) Still in Zhejiang, at local level, the Jiaxing Municipality released a Three-Year-Action Plan [^ntr111-L_202602512EN.000101-E0111] seeking to ‘implement a plan to foster leading technology enterprises [… and] encourage leading enterprises in traditional industries such as textile materials and chemical materials to increase their innovation and R & D efforts’.

- (178) Satellite Chemicals Co. Ltd. is located in the Jiaxing Economic and Technological Development Zone [^ntr112-L_202602512EN.000101-E0112].

- (179) Similarly, the Shanghai 14th FYP for the Development of Chemical Industry Parks [^ntr113-L_202602512EN.000101-E0113] mandates to ‘optimize the industrial structure, enhance industrial innovation capabilities, improve the industrial chain, enrich the industrial development ecosystem, and further enhance core competitiveness, in accordance with national and Shanghai municipal strategic needs, as well as with the strategy of strengthening the leading role of high-end industries, with a particular focus on the chemical manufacturing industry’.

- (180) Furthermore, Shanghai Huayi Group benefitted from the ‘long term support in raw material and electricity supply’ [^ntr114-L_202602512EN.000101-E0114] from China Energy Group, another SOE under SASAC [^ntr115-L_202602512EN.000101-E0115] and both SOEs signed a strategic cooperation agreement in 2021 [^ntr116-L_202602512EN.000101-E0116]. Through these and other means, the GOC therefore directs and controls virtually every aspect in the development and functioning of the sector, as well as the upstream inputs and the downstream products.

- (181) In sum, the GOC has measures in place to induce operators to comply with the public policy objectives concerning the sector. Such measures impede market forces from operating freely.

###### 3.2.3.5.   Significant distortions according to Article 2(6a)(b), fourth indent of the basic Regulation: the lack, discriminatory application or inadequate enforcement of bankruptcy, corporate or property laws

- (182) According to the information on file, the Chinese bankruptcy system delivers inadequately on its own main objectives such as to fairly settle claims and debts and to safeguard the lawful rights and interests of creditors and debtors. This appears to be rooted in the fact that while the Chinese bankruptcy law formally rests on principles that are similar to those applied in corresponding laws in countries other than China, the Chinese system is characterised by systematic under-enforcement.

- (183) The number of bankruptcies remains notoriously low in relation to the size of the country’s economy, not least because the insolvency proceedings suffer from a number of shortcomings which effectively function as a disincentive for bankruptcy filings. Moreover, the role of the state in the insolvency proceedings remains strong and active, often having direct influence on the outcome of the proceedings [^ntr117-L_202602512EN.000101-E0117].

- (184) In addition, the shortcomings of the system of property rights are particularly obvious in relation to ownership of land and land-use rights in China [^ntr118-L_202602512EN.000101-E0118]. All land is owned by the state (collectively owned rural land and State-owned urban land) and its allocation remains solely dependent on the state. There are legal provisions that aim at allocating land use rights in a transparent manner and at market prices, for instance by introducing bidding procedures. However, these provisions are regularly not respected, with certain buyers obtaining their land for free or below market rates [^ntr119-L_202602512EN.000101-E0119]. Moreover, authorities often pursue specific political goals including the implementation of the economic plans when allocating land [^ntr120-L_202602512EN.000101-E0120].

- (185) Much like other sectors in the Chinese economy, the producers of the product concerned are subject to the ordinary rules on Chinese bankruptcy, corporate, and property laws. That has the effect that these companies, too, are subject to the top-down distortions arising from the discriminatory application or inadequate enforcement of bankruptcy and property laws. Those considerations, on the basis of the evidence available, appear to be fully applicable also in the acrylic esters sector. The present investigation revealed nothing that would call those findings into question.

- (186) In light of the above, the Commission concluded that there was discriminatory application or inadequate enforcement of bankruptcy and property laws in the sector of the product concerned.

###### 3.2.3.6.   Significant distortions according to Article 2(6a)(b), fifth indent of the basic Regulation: wage costs being distorted

- (187) A system of market-based wages cannot fully develop in China as workers and employers are impeded in their rights to collective organisation. China has not ratified a number of essential conventions of the International Labour Organisation, in particular those on freedom of association and on collective bargaining [^ntr121-L_202602512EN.000101-E0121].

- (188) Under national law, only one trade union organisation is active. However, this organisation lacks independence from the state authorities and its engagement in collective bargaining and protection of workers’ rights remains rudimentary [^ntr122-L_202602512EN.000101-E0122]. Moreover, the mobility of the Chinese workforce is restricted by the household registration system, which limits access to the full range of social security and other benefits to local residents of a given administrative area.

- (189) This typically results in workers who are not in possession of the local residence registration finding themselves in a vulnerable employment position and receiving lower income than the holders of the residence registration [^ntr123-L_202602512EN.000101-E0123]. Those findings lead to the distortion of wage costs in China.

- (190) No evidence was submitted to the effect that the acrylic esters. sector would not be subject to the Chinese labour law system described. The sector is thus affected by the distortions of wage costs both directly (when making the product concerned or the main raw material for its production) as well as indirectly (when having access to capital or inputs from companies subject to the same labour system in China).

###### 3.2.3.7.   Significant distortions according to Article 2(6a)(b), sixth indent of the basic Regulation: access to finance granted by institutions which implement public policy objectives or otherwise not acting independently of the state

- (191) Access to capital for corporate actors in China is subject to various distortions.

- (192) First, the Chinese financial system is characterised by the strong position of state-owned banks [^ntr124-L_202602512EN.000101-E0124], which, when granting access to finance, take into consideration criteria other than the economic viability of a project. Similar to non-financial SOEs, the banks remain connected to the state not only through ownership but also via personal relations (the top executives of large state-owned financial institutions are ultimately appointed by the CCP) [^ntr125-L_202602512EN.000101-E0125] and they regularly implement public policies designed by the GOC.

- (193) In doing so, the banks comply with an explicit legal obligation to conduct their business in accordance with the needs of the national economic and social development and under the guidance of the industrial policies of the state [^ntr126-L_202602512EN.000101-E0126]. While it is acknowledged that various legal provisions refer to the need to respect normal banking behaviour and prudential rules such as the need to examine the creditworthiness of the borrower, the overwhelming evidence, including findings made in trade defence investigations, suggests that these provisions play only a secondary role in the application of the various legal instruments.

- (194) Recent developments further illustrate the extent of government influence over financial institutions in China. In March 2025, the Government of China (GOC) announced an issuance of RMB 500 billion in treasury bonds to provide substantial financial support to major banks, including the Bank of China, China Construction Bank, Bank of Communications, and Postal Savings Bank of China. This intervention was aimed at stabilising these institutions amidst declining profitability and record low net interest margins, highlighting the proactive measures taken by the state to maintain economic stability [^ntr127-L_202602512EN.000101-E0127].

- (195) Also, the GOC has clarified that even private commercial banking decisions must be overseen by the CCP and remain in line with national policies. One of the state’s three overarching goals in relation to banking governance is now to strengthen the Party’s leadership in the banking and insurance sector, including in relation to operational and management issues [^ntr128-L_202602512EN.000101-E0128]. Also, the performance evaluation criteria of commercial banks have now to, notably, take into account how entities ‘serve the national development objectives and the real economy’, and in particular how they ‘serve strategic and emerging industries’ [^ntr129-L_202602512EN.000101-E0129].

- (196) Furthermore, on the level of allocation of financial resources, with Several Measures to Further Promote the Development of Private Investment [^ntr130-L_202602512EN.000101-E0130], the GOC seeks to ‘increase central budget resources to support qualified private investment projects and to actively play a guiding and leading role’. The GOC also intends to ‘make good use of new policy financial instruments [and] support a number of qualified private investment projects in important industries and key areas’ [^ntr131-L_202602512EN.000101-E0131].

- (197) Additionally, bond and credit ratings are often distorted for a variety of reasons including the fact that the risk assessment is influenced by the firm’s strategic importance to the GOC and the strength of any implicit guarantee by the government [^ntr132-L_202602512EN.000101-E0132]. This is compounded by additional existing rules, which direct finances into sectors designated by the government as encouraged or otherwise important [^ntr133-L_202602512EN.000101-E0133]. This results in a bias in favour of lending to SOEs, large well-connected private firms and firms in key industrial sectors, which implies that the availability and cost of capital is not equal for all players on the market.

- (198) Second, borrowing costs have been kept artificially low to stimulate investment growth. This has led to the excessive use of capital investment with ever lower returns on investment. This is illustrated by the growth in corporate leverage in the state sector despite a sharp fall in profitability, which suggests that the mechanisms at work in the banking system do not follow normal commercial responses.

- (199) Thirdly, although nominal interest rate liberalization was achieved in October 2015, price signals are still not the result of free market forces but are influenced by government-induced distortions. The share of lending at or below the benchmark rate still represented at least one-third of all lending as of the end of 2018 [^ntr134-L_202602512EN.000101-E0134]. Official media in China have recently reported that the CCP called for ‘guiding the loan market interest rate downwards’ [^ntr135-L_202602512EN.000101-E0135]. Artificially low interest rates result in under-pricing, and consequently, the excessive utilization of capital.

- (200) Overall credit growth in China indicates a worsening efficiency of capital allocation without any signs of credit tightening that would be expected in an undistorted market environment. As a result, non-performing loans have increased rapidly, with the GOC a number of times opting to either avoid defaults, thus creating so called ‘zombie’ companies, or to transfer the ownership of the debt (e.g. via mergers or debt-to-equity swaps), without necessarily removing the overall debt problem or addressing its root causes.

- (201) In essence, despite the steps that have been taken to liberalize the market, the corporate credit system in China is affected by significant distortions resulting from the continuing pervasive role of the state in the capital markets. Therefore, the substantial government intervention in the financial system leads to the market conditions being severely affected at all levels.

- (202) No evidence was submitted in the present investigation demonstrating that the sector of the product concerned is not affected by the government intervention in the financial system in the sense of Article 2(6a)(b), sixth indent of the basic Regulation. Therefore, the substantial government intervention in the financial system leads to the market conditions being severely affected at all levels.

###### 3.2.3.8.   Systemic nature of the distortions described

- (203) The Commission noted that the distortions described in the updated Report are characteristic for the Chinese economy. The evidence available shows that the facts and features of the Chinese system as described above as well as in Part I of the updated Report apply throughout the country and across the sectors of the economy. The same holds true for the description of the factors of production as set out above and in Part II of the updated Report.

- (204) The Commission recalls that in order to produce the product concerned, certain inputs are needed. When the producers of the product concerned purchase/contract these inputs, the prices they pay (and which are recorded as their costs) are clearly exposed to the same systemic distortions mentioned before. For instance, suppliers of inputs employ labour that is subject to the distortions. They may borrow money that is subject to the distortions on the financial sector/capital allocation. In addition, they are subject to the planning system that applies across all levels of government and sectors. These distortions were described in detail above, in particular in recitals (141) to (202). The Commission pointed out that the regulatory setup underpinning those distortions is generally applicable, acrylic esters producers being subject to those rules as any other economic operator in China. The distortions have therefore a direct bearing on the cost structure of the product concerned.

- (205) As a consequence, not only the domestic sales prices of the product concerned are not appropriate for use within the meaning of Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation, but all the input costs (including raw materials, energy, land, financing, labour, etc.) are also affected because their price formation is affected by substantial government intervention, as described in Parts I and II of the updated Report.

- (206) Indeed, the government interventions described in relation to the allocation of capital, land, labour, energy and raw materials are present throughout China. This means, for instance, that an input that in itself was produced in China by combining a range of factors of production is exposed to significant distortions. The same applies for the input to the input and so forth.

- (207) No evidence or argument to the contrary has been adduced by the GOC or the exporting producers in the present investigation.

##### 3.2.4.   *Representative country*

###### 3.2.4.1.   General remarks

- (208) The choice of the representative country was based on the following criteria pursuant to Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation:

- — A level of economic development similar to China. For this purpose, the Commission used countries with a gross national income per capita similar to China on the basis of the database of the World Bank [^ntr136-L_202602512EN.000101-E0136];

- — Production of the product under investigation in that country;

- — Existence of relevant readily available data in the representative country.

- — Where there is more than one possible representative country, preference was given, where appropriate, to the country with an adequate level of social and environmental protection.

- (209) As explained in recital (103) above, the Commission issued one note (‘the FOP Note’) on the sources for the determination of the normal value.

- (210) This note described the facts and evidence underlying the relevant criteria.

- (211) The note also informed interested parties of the Commission’s intention to consider Indonesia as an appropriate representative country in the present case if the existence of significant distortions pursuant to Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation would be confirmed.

###### 3.2.4.2.   A level of economic development similar to China

- (212) In the FOP note, the Commission identified Brazil, Malaysia, Indonesia and South Africa where production of the product under investigation was known to take place and as countries with a similar level of economic development as China according to the World Bank, as they are all classified as ‘upper-middle income’ countries on a gross national income basis.

- (213) The complainant argued that Indonesia did not consistently meet the criterion of a level of economic development similar to that of China, as its World Bank income classification had fluctuated between lower-middle-income and upper-middle-income status in recent years. The complainant submitted that, unlike Brazil, which had consistently remained classified as an upper-middle-income country, Indonesia lacked the stability and consistency required for selection as the representative country under Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation.

- (214) The Commission rejected this claim and pointed out that during the investigation period, Indonesia was classified by the World Bank as an upper-middle-income country, corresponding to the level of economic development considered appropriate for the purposes of the representative country analysis. Accordingly, the Commission considered that Indonesia satisfied the criterion set out in Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation.

###### 3.2.4.3.   Existence of relevant readily available data in the representative country

- (215) In the FOP Note, the Commission informed the interested parties that it intended to use Indonesia as an appropriate representative country and Intanwijaya Internasional, in accordance with Article 2(6a)(a), first indent of the basic Regulation in order to source undistorted prices or benchmarks for the calculation of normal value.

- (216) The Commission assessed in the FOP Note the other representative countries identified by the complainant and the Commission besides Indonesia, i.e. Brazil, Malaysia, and South Africa, and did not find them to be suitable.

- (217) With regards to Brazil, based on the Global Trade Atlas [^ntr137-L_202602512EN.000101-E0137] (‘GTA’) there were only negligible imports of propylene (i.e. 40 tonnes) which alone represented 30 to 35 % of the cost of production of acrylic esters. Moreover, Brazil imported from China 90 % of these 40 MT of propylene and 50 % of acrylic acid in the investigation period. The Commission therefore considered Brazil not to be an appropriate representative country.

- (218) With regards to Malaysia, based on the GTA, there were only negligible imports of n-butanol (i.e. 63 tonnes) which alone represented 40 to 45 % of the cost of production of the total acrylic esters and up to 50 to 55 % of the cost of production of the Butyl Acrylate (BA) type. Moreover, Malaysia imported from China 30 % of these 63 MT n-butanol, 50 % of acrylic acid and 20 % of 2- Ethylhexanol in the IP. The Commission therefore considered Malaysia not to be an appropriate representative country.

- (219) With regards to South Africa, based on the GTA, there were essentially non-existent imports of propylene (i.e. 5 tonnes) and n-butanol (i.e. 369 tonnes) which are by far the most important raw materials to produce acrylic esters. Moreover, South Africa imported from China 69 % of the propylene and 98 % of acrylic acid in the IP. The Commission therefore considered South Africa not to be an appropriate representative country.

- (220) With regards to Indonesia, based on the GTA, imports statistics showed that the country imported the highest amount in tonnes of all the raw materials listed in Table 1 during the investigation period. Out of the total quantity imported, Indonesia imported from China less than 15 % of acrylic acid, less than 6 % of 2- ethylhexanol and close to 0 % of propylene, n-butanol and propane.

- (221) Based on the information available to the Commission, there was only one producer manufacturing acrylic esters in Indonesia, PT. Nippon Shokubai Indonesia. This company was part of Nippon Shokubai (Japan) and it was one of the major producers of acrylic esters in Asia. However, it only presented consolidated annual accounts at global level [^ntr138-L_202602512EN.000101-E0138]. It was therefore not possible to extract the financial statements for acrylic esters or for the Indonesian branch of the company.

- (222) One of the sampled Chinese exporting producers proposed to use the company PT Chandra Asri Pacific TBK. The Commission assessed the suggested company. The company does not produce acrylic esters, but upstream products (including propylene, one of the main raw materials for acrylic esters). Based on the information provided in Orbis [^ntr139-L_202602512EN.000101-E0139], financial data for this company were available for 2025 and at consolidated level the company was profitable in 2025. However, when looking at data for the chemical department, the company was lossmaking in 2025 [^ntr140-L_202602512EN.000101-E0140].

- (223) The Commission did not identify any other readily available financial statements of other producers of acrylic esters in Indonesia and considered the producers of a product in the same general category or sector of the product under investigation. Therefore, the Commission looked at other companies within the same NACE code as acrylic esters (i.e. NACE 2014, ‘Manufacture of organic chemical raw materials’). The Commission found a company, Intanwijaya Internasional, which produces formaldehyde. It is noteworthy that formaldehyde shares a very similar production process with the acrylaldehyde, which is the result of the first step of acrylic esters manufacturing, when propylene is reacted with oxygen. This company was profitable in 2025, and its financial data were publicly available on Orbis.

- (224) Interested parties were invited to comment on the appropriateness of Indonesia as a representative country and of Intanwijaya Internasional as producer in the representative country.

- (225) The complainant argued that Indonesia was not an appropriate representative country because Indonesia's benchmarks were influenced by regional cost advantages, including ASEAN preferential trade arrangements, lower labour and electricity costs, and import prices for key raw materials that were allegedly affected by regional pricing dynamics, particularly with Malaysia and other ASEAN suppliers. In particular, the complainant argued that imports into Indonesia of n-Butanol were mostly from Malaysia and that these were impacted by preferential tariff arrangements between ASEAN members. Moreover, the complainant noted that propylene import prices into Indonesia and more generally in the Southeast Asian market, moved broadly in line with Chinese prices. According to the complainant, the Brazilian benchmarks better reflected undistorted international market conditions and should therefore be preferred.

- (226) The Commission rejected these claims. The complainant did not provide sufficient evidence demonstrating that the Indonesian benchmarks were distorted within the meaning of Article 2(6a) of the basic Regulation. The mere fact that the Indonesian benchmark values were lower than those proposed for Brazil does not establish that they are distorted or unsuitable for the construction of normal value. Lower production costs may legitimately arise from market-based factors such as geographical location, regional supply integration, logistics efficiencies or labour market conditions and cannot, in themselves, justify the rejection of otherwise reliable benchmark data.

- (227) As regards n-Butanol, the Commission noted that the complainant's arguments on preferential tariff treatment do not, by itself, demonstrate that the underlying transaction prices are not market based. Nor did the complainant provide evidence that Malaysian export prices were subject to government intervention or otherwise failed to reflect normal commercial conditions. The Commission therefore considered that the complainant had not substantiated their claim that the Indonesian import prices were unsuitable for benchmark purposes.

- (228) As regards propylene, the Commission considered that such price correlation is not uncommon for internationally traded commodities and does not, in itself, demonstrate that Indonesian import prices are distorted or determined by Chinese domestic market conditions. The complainant did not provide evidence demonstrating that the Indonesian benchmark prices did not reflect market-based transactions.

- (229) The complainant argued that Brazil should not have been excluded as a representative country solely because of its low propylene import volumes. They submitted that Brazil's limited imports reflected its substantial domestic production capacity rather than a lack of representative market conditions and that the Commission should instead have relied on alternative undistorted benchmarks, such as publicly available ICIS prices, in accordance with Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation.

- (230) The Commission did not accept this claim. The representative country assessment is based on the availability of appropriate and reliable readily available data for the factors of production. The complainant did not provide sufficient evidence demonstrating that the proposed alternative benchmark accurately reflected undistorted costs in Brazil or that it was more appropriate than the benchmark data available for the selected representative country. Accordingly, the Commission considered that the existence of domestic production capacity in Brazil did not alter its assessment.

- (231) The complainant further criticised the choice of Indonesia as representative country due to absence of Indonesian producer of acrylic esters with readily available financial statements and that the Commission had to rely on a producer of formaldehyde as a proxy for SG & A and profit. According to the complainant, formaldehyde production was not technically or economically comparable to acrylic esters manufacturing, and the absence of financial data from an actual or closely comparable producer constituted an additional reason why Indonesia was not a suitable representative country under Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation.

- (232) The Commission rejected this claim. As explained in recitals (215) to (223), the selection of the representative country was based on the criteria set out in Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation and the availability of the best relevant readily available information.

- (233) While the Commission acknowledged that no standalone financial statements of an Indonesian producer of the product under investigation were readily available, the complainants did not identify any alternative Indonesian company that would be more comparable, nor did they submit any alternative representative country that would provide more appropriate relevant readily available financial information while satisfying the relevant selection criteria.

- (234) Accordingly, the Commission considered that the use of the selected proxy company did not preclude the selection of Indonesia as the representative country.

- (235) In the alternative, the complainant proposed a hybrid methodology for the construction of normal value. They argued that the Commission should use Brazil as the source of the factor of production benchmarks, while relying on the financial statements of Intanwijaya Internasional in Indonesia for the calculation of SG & A and profit.

- (236) They submitted that Article 2(6a)(a) of the basic Regulation and the Commission’s previous practice allowed the use of different undistorted sources for different elements of the constructed normal value, where this results in more appropriate benchmark data.

- (237) The Commission noted that there was no need for such an approach as all data necessary to construct the normal value was available in the appropriate representative country. Indeed, as explained in recitals (215) to (223), the Commission established that appropriate readily available data were available in Indonesia for the construction of the normal value.

- (238) Finally, the complainant argued that while applying the methodology explained in recital (223) and selecting a company within the same NACE code as acrylic esters, the Commission should have also selected another active producer of formaldehyde in Indonesia (i.e. PT Duta Pertiwi Nusantara Tbk) with publicly available standalone financial statements for 2025, alongside Intanwijaya Internasional.

- (239) The Commission analysed the 2025 financial accounts of PT Duta Pertiwi Nusantara Tbk and concluded that the company could not be regarded as a source of undistorted and reasonable amounts for SG & A costs and for profit, since its financial performance did not reflect normal operating conditions. According to its published audited accounts, the company permanently ceased production in February 2025 [^ntr141-L_202602512EN.000101-E0141], resulting in revenue falling by approximately 84 % compared with 2024; SG & A expenses amounted to around 100 % of net sales, reflecting fixed overheads spread over a reduced sales base; and the net result was primarily attributable to non-operating income. The claim was therefore rejected.

###### 3.2.4.4.   Level of social and environmental protection

- (240) Having established that Indonesia was the only available appropriate representative country, based on all of the above elements, there was no need to carry out an assessment of the level of social and environmental protection in accordance with the last sentence of Article 2(6a)(a) first indent of the basic Regulation.

###### 3.2.4.5.   Conclusion

- (241) In view of the above analysis, Indonesia met the criteria laid down in Article 2(6a)(a), first indent of the basic Regulation to be considered as an appropriate representative country.

###### 3.2.6.3.   Labour

- (252) In the FOP Note, the Commission indicated that it would use the information provided by ERI SalaryExpert [^ntr143-L_202602512EN.000101-E0143] on the average salary for chemical workers in Indonesia, based on salary surveys collected from Indonesian employers and employees. It also indicated that it would add the mandatory additional labour related costs borne by the employer, including the work accident insurance (JKK), the old age benefit (JHT), the life insurance (JKM), the contribution to the pension scheme (JP) and the national health insurance (BPJS) [^ntr144-L_202602512EN.000101-E0144].

- (253) Following the FOP Note, the complainant argued that the Commission underestimated the actual Indonesian employers’ labour costs. In particular:

- (254) The complainant disagreed with the Commission's use of an average rate for the JKK, which can range from 0,24 % to 1,74 % of wages, depending on how dangerous the job is. Since acrylic esters production is a high-risk petrochemical process, the JKK rate should be applied at the maximum rate (1,74 %), corresponding to the highest risk category in the BPJS risk classification [^ntr145-L_202602512EN.000101-E0145], rather than at a lower rate within the 0,24 %–1,74 % range.

- (255) The religious holiday bonus (THR) and the contribution to the unemployment insurance programme (JKP) were not included among the mandatory additional labour related costs paid by employers.

- (256) The Commission relied on a national average labour cost for chemical workers. Instead, it should have applied either the sectoral minimum wage for Banten, where the sole identified Indonesian producer, PT Nippon Shokubai Indonesia, is located, multiplied by 2,5 to reflect experienced worker pay levels, or specific data drawn from BPS’s Statistics of Indonesia Manufacturing Industry 2023 [^ntr146-L_202602512EN.000101-E0146] publication.

- (257) First, acrylic esters production is not explicitly listed in the BPJS risk classification. However, according to Indonesia’s official standard industrial classification system (KBLI) [^ntr147-L_202602512EN.000101-E0147], the basic chemical industry from petroleum, natural gas and coal is classified as a high-risk industry. Therefore, given its hazardous nature, the Commission considered it plausible that acrylic esters production fell under the BPJS highest risk category. On this basis, the Commission agreed with the complainant that the maximum rate of 1,74 % should be applied in the calculation of the hourly labour cost, rather than an average.

- (258) Second, the Commission found that the THR, equivalent to one full month's gross wage, was indeed a mandatory charge [^ntr148-L_202602512EN.000101-E0148], and should therefore be added to the calculation of the labour cost. However, the Commission found that the JKP was not an additional cost for the employer, since the employer-funded portion of the JKP was included in the JKK contribution itself, not paid separately on top of it [^ntr149-L_202602512EN.000101-E0149]. Therefore, no addition for JKP was warranted.

- (259) Last, the Commission considered that national averages remained a reliable benchmark as the data pooled by ERI SalaryExpert were actual data from a broad and representative dataset of chemical workers. Moreover, the proposed 2,5 multiplier for experienced worker pay, to be applied on sectoral minimum wages, was unsubstantiated, and in any event the resulting figure would not depart considerably from the salary level used by the Commission. As regards the suggested use of specific data from BPS’s *Statistics of Indonesia Manufacturing Industry 2023* publication, the Commission was unable to identify the relevant data within that source, nor did the complainant provide it.

###### 3.2.6.4.   Electricity

- (260) In the FOP Note, the Commission indicated that it intended to use the price of electricity for companies (industrial users) in Indonesia as published by GlobalPetrolPrices. The Commission used the published business electricity price for December 2025 [^ntr150-L_202602512EN.000101-E0150]. The tariff of GlobalPetrolPrices was based on the energy rate of the sole Indonesian provider Perusahaan Listrik Negara (PLN).

- (261) Following the FOP Note, the complainant argued that the PLN was subsidised by the Indonesian government and its electricity tariff was therefore not a genuine undistorted market price.

- (262) The complainant also claimed that the price from GlobalPetrolPrices.com reflected only the base energy tariff, but did not include additional mandatory billing components like demand charges, time-of-use peak multipliers, reactive power penalties, and VAT.

- (263) The Commission found that Indonesian electricity tariffs were divided into subsidised and non-subsidised categories. While subsidised tariff rates were intended for small and disadvantaged households with low voltage needs [^ntr151-L_202602512EN.000101-E0151], the industrial tariff used by the Commission and applicable to medium-voltage industrial consumers was not identified as a subsidised tariff. The Commission therefore considered that the existence of subsidies for certain PLN customers did not establish that the industrial tariff used by the Commission was subsidised or distorted. The claim was therefore rejected.

- (264) As to the second claim, the Commission analysed the tariff rates for industrial users (category I-3) as published by PLN [^ntr152-L_202602512EN.000101-E0152]. For this category, the additional billing components are inapplicable in practice.

- (265) There is no standing demand charge: the tariff only includes a minimum-billing floor equal to 40 hours of consumption at the contracted capacity, which is not triggered under normal industrial utilisation levels.

- (266) No time-of-use surcharge is being charged, since PLN currently bills peak and off-peak hours at the same rate.

- (267) Reactive energy is the electricity needed to sustain alternating electromagnetic field in inductive equipment like motors and transformers, without doing any direct mechanical work. It still uses grid capacity and therefore electricity providers charge extra when a facility uses too much of it relative to its real electricity consumption. Indonesia sets its no-penalty threshold at a power factor [^ntr153-L_202602512EN.000101-E0153] of 0,85. China’s own regulations set a stricter standard and require industrial users to maintain a power factor of 0,90 [^ntr154-L_202602512EN.000101-E0154]. Applying Indonesia’s own compliance threshold, a facility maintaining the power factor required under China’s own rules would incur no reactive-energy surcharge.

- (268) Lastly, VAT is a fully recoverable input tax for a VAT-registered industrial purchaser. The claims were therefore rejected.

- (269) However, the Commission recalculated the electricity benchmark using the tariff published by PLN but adjusting it to reflect the mandatory regional street-lighting tax [^ntr155-L_202602512EN.000101-E0155], calculated as a percentage of the billed electricity amount.

#### 3.3.   **The United States of America**

##### 3.3.1.   *Normal value*

- (296) One exporting producer, The Dow Chemical Company Group (‘Dow US’), comprising the producers Rohm & Haas (R & H) and Union Carbide Corporation (UCC), cooperated with the investigation. No other exporting producers in the USA, that exported during the IP to the EU, were identified.

- (297) The Commission first examined whether Dow US’s total volume of domestic sales was representative, in accordance with Article 2(2) of the basic Regulation. The domestic sales are representative if the total domestic sales volume of the like product to independent customers on the domestic market per exporting producer represented at least 5 % of its total export sales volume of the product concerned to the Union during the investigation period. On that basis, the total sales of Dow US of the like product on the domestic market were representative.

- (298) The Commission subsequently identified the product types sold domestically by Dow US that were identical or comparable with the product types sold for export to the Union with representative domestic sales.

- (299) The Commission then examined whether the domestic sales by Dow US on its domestic market for each product type that is identical or comparable with a product type sold for export to the Union were representative, in accordance with Article 2(2) of the basic Regulation. The domestic sales of a product type are representative if the total volume of domestic sales of that product type to independent customers during the investigation period represents at least 5 % of the total volume of export sales of the identical or comparable product type to the Union. The Commission established that the domestic sales of each product type were representative for the exporting producer.

- (300) The Commission next defined the proportion of profitable sales to independent customers in the domestic market for each product type during the investigation period in order to decide whether to use actual domestic sales for the calculation of the normal value, in accordance with Article 2(4) of the basic Regulation.

- (301) The normal value is based on the actual domestic price per product type, irrespective of whether those sales are profitable or not, if:

- (a) the sales volume of the product type, sold at a net sales price equal to or above the calculated cost of production, represented more than 80 % of the total sales volume of that product type; and

- (b) the weighted average sales price of that product type is equal to or higher than the unit cost of production.

- (302) In this case, the normal value is the weighted average of the prices of all domestic sales of that product type during the IP.

- (303) The normal value is the actual domestic price per product type of only the profitable domestic sales of the product types during the IP, if:

- (a) the volume of profitable sales of the product type represents 80 % or less of the total sales volume of that type; or

- (b) the weighted average price of this product type is below the unit cost of production.

- (304) The analysis of domestic sales showed that less than 80 % of all domestic sales of the product types that are identical or comparable with the product types sold for export to the Union were profitable. Accordingly, for all product types, the normal value was calculated as actual domestic price per product type of only the profitable domestic sales of the product types during the IP.

##### 3.3.2.   *Export price*

- (305) Dow Chemical exported to the Union indirectly through Dow Europe, a related importer located in Switzerland.

- (306) The export price for sales made through Dow Europe was established on the basis of the price at which the imported product was first resold to independent customers in the Union, in accordance with Article 2(9) of the basic Regulation. In this case, adjustments to the price were made for all costs incurred between importation and resale, including SG & A costs, and for profits accruing.

- (307) The profit of Dow Europe was found to be fully affected by the association between the two companies as it was directly allocated by Dow US. The transfer price therefore was not a price that would have been established in a negotiation with an independent importer. The Commission therefore concluded that the profit margin of Dow Europe was not reasonable. Pursuant to Article 2(9) of the basic Regulation, it is considered appropriate to use a reasonable profit margin independent of the actual profit resulting from the transfer price in order to avoid any distorting effects that may arise from the transfer price, in line with established case-law of Union courts.

- (308) Two unrelated importers cooperated with the investigation, but were loss-making. In the absence of any reasonable profit provided by the cooperating unrelated importers, the Commission decided to resort to the profit margin used in a previous proceeding concerning another chemical product manufactured by a similar industry and imported under similar circumstances, namely a profit margin of 6,89 % [^ntr158-L_202602512EN.000101-E0158] established in the polyvinyl alcohols (‘PVA’) investigation. In the Commission’s view that profit margin is the most objective basis available for the purpose of arriving at a satisfactory estimate of an arm's length export price.

##### 3.3.3.   *Comparison*

- (309) Article 2(10) of the basic Regulation requires the Commission to make a fair comparison between the normal value and the export price at the same level of trade and to make allowances for differences in factors which affect prices and price comparability. In the case at hand the Commission chose to compare the normal value and the export price of the sampled exporting producers at the *ex-works* level of trade. #### 4.1.   **Definition of the Union industry and Union production**

- (338) The like product was manufactured by four producers in the Union during the investigation period. They constitute the ‘Union industry’ within the meaning of Article 4(1) of the basic Regulation.

- (339) The total Union production during the investigation period was established at around 490 000 tonnes. #### 4.2.   **Determination of the relevant Union market**

- (340) To establish whether the Union industry suffered injury and to determine consumption and the various economic indicators related to the situation of the Union industry, the Commission examined whether and to what extent the subsequent use of the Union industry’s production of the like product had to be taken into account in the analysis.

- (341) The Commission found that a substantial part of the sampled Union producers’ AE production was destined for captive use (around 55% over the period considered) by the Union producers or their related companies for the downstream production of acrylic dispersions or additives. The product was often simply transferred (without invoice) and/or delivered at transfer prices within the same group of companies (which did not have a free choice of supplier) for further downstream processing.

- (342) The distinction between captive and free market is relevant for the injury analysis because products destined for captive use are not exposed to direct competition from imports. By contrast, production destined for free market sale is in direct competition with imports of the product concerned.

- (343) To provide a picture of the Union industry that is as complete as possible, the Commission obtained data for the entire AE activity and determined whether the production was destined for captive use or for the free market.

- (344) The Commission examined certain economic indicators relating to the Union industry on the basis of data for the free market. These indicators are sales volume and sales prices on the Union market; market share; growth; export volume and prices; profitability; return on investment; and cash flow. Where justified, the findings of the examination were compared with the data for the captive market to provide a more complete picture of the situation of the Union industry.

- (345) Other economic indicators could meaningfully be examined only by referring to the whole activity, including the captive use of the Union industry. These are: production; capacity, capacity utilisation; investments; stocks; employment; productivity; wages; and ability to raise capital. They depend on the whole activity, whether the production is captive or sold on the free market.

#### 4.4.   **Imports from the countries concerned**

##### 4.4.1.   *Cumulative assessment of the effects of imports from the countries concerned*

- (350) The Commission examined whether imports of AE originating in the countries concerned should be assessed cumulatively, in accordance with Article 3(4) of the basic Regulation.

- (351) The margin of dumping established in relation to the imports from all countries concerned was above the de minimis threshold laid down in Article 9(3) of the basic Regulation. The volume of imports from each of the countries concerned was not negligible within the meaning of Article 5(7) of the basic Regulation. Market shares in the investigation period were [11–13] % for the US, [8–10] % for China, [4–6] % for South Africa and [4–6] % for Saudi Arabia.

- (352) The conditions of competition between the dumped imports from the countries concerned and between the dumped imports from the countries concerned and the like product were similar. More specifically, the imported AE competed with each other and with the AE produced in the Union because they were sold ultimately to industrial users. Furthermore, the imports from the countries concerned were all in competition with each other and the Union production, because their pricing was based on ICIS [^ntr159-L_202602512EN.000101-E0159] indices. The imports all consisted of AE sharing the same basic characteristics meaning that they were fully interchangeable. AE are considered commodity products that are comparable regardless of their origin. Moreover, imports from all the countries concerned followed largely similar price patterns over the entirety of the period considered.

- (353) Therefore, all the criteria set out in Article 3(4) of the basic Regulation were met and imports from all the countries concerned were examined cumulatively for the purposes of the injury determination.

- (354) Dow argued that the Commission should not cumulate imports from the USA with imports from the other countries concerned, in particular, China. Dow claimed that the USA imports were consistently present in Union even prior to the period considered (unlike for example Chinese imports), they declined in the period considered and represented a small share of the Union consumption. Dow also claimed that import from the USA were not subject to similar conditions of competition as imports from China, as EU market prices were driven by the exceptionally low prices of Chinese imports of AE.

- (355) Moreover, Dow maintained that only a limited volume of imports of AE from the USA is sold on the Union free market and most of its imports are used captively, while these captive volumes do not compete with free market sales and do not exert price pressure on the EU market.

- (356) As outlined in recitals (351)-(352), conditions for cumulative assessment were met for the USA imports and hence the USA imports were assessed together with imports from the other countries concerned and were not subject to individual assessment in this case. Neither continued presence on the Union market prior to the period considered, nor the alleged evolution of import volumes or prices (which in any event followed the same price pattern as import prices from other countries concerned) over the period considered could invalidate this determination in the fact of the findings made regarding the similarity of conditions of competition.

- (357) In relation to the nature of USA imports entering the Union, for the purposes of this case and for the evaluation of macro-indicators, all USA imports were treated as imports that are sold or capable to be sold on the free market. In this respect, the Commission noted that imports, whether captive or not, are in direct competition with the free sales of the Union industry and may cause injury to them, as each import represents a potentially lost sale. Furthermore, contrary to what Dow US claimed, their exports were sold in significant quantities ([15–25] kt) on the Union free market. In view of the above the claim was rejected

- (358) Gantrade Europe BV (‘Gantrade’), a cooperating unrelated importer, argued that the conditions for the cumulative assessment of imports from the countries concerned were not met in the present case. According to Gantrade, given the differences in captive use (large part of USA exports is for captive use), tariff treatment (South Africa benefits from 0 % customs duty as opposed to imports from Saudi Arabia, the USA and China), import trends, and production methods (the South African producer uses coal-based chemistry for AE production), the conditions of competition were not comparable across the countries concerned.

- (359) Moreover, Sasol claimed that imports from South Africa should not be cumulated with those from the other countries concerned because of different competition conditions. More specifically, Sasol submitted that imports from South Africa do not compete with China, the United States, as well as the Union industry across the full scope of product types covered by the investigation, since South Africa only exported to the Union one AE product type (BA), which moreover does not have similar physical characteristics, nor is interchangeable with the other AE type (2EHA). Furthermore, Sasol also observed that South African import volumes were markedly lower relative to other countries concerned and that the South African prices followed prevailing market conditions rather than setting the prices.

- (360) Finally, according to Dow, imports from the USA were not subject to similar conditions of competition because import prices from the USA followed, rather than set, market prices.

- (361) Regarding Gantrade’s claims, it was established that all imports, whether captive or not, are in direct competition with the free sales of the Union industry and may cause injury to them, as each import represents a potentially lost sale. Furthermore, differences in production methods or in tariff treatment of certain imports are not factors materially affecting the determination as to whether conditions for cumulative assessment under Article 3(4)(b) are met. Instead, focus is on the overall conditions of competition in terms of pricing behaviour, sales channels, customers and interchangeability of the products imported and sold by the Union producers and the exporting producers in the other exporting countries.

- (362) With respect to Sasol’s claim, the fact that South Africa only imported one AE product type equally did not invalidate the conclusions drawn by the Commission. The product type imported by South Africa (BA) was the same as that produced and sold on the Union market by other countries concerned as well as by the Union industry. Therefore, South Africa’s imports were in competition with other imports and the Union industry. The fact that Chinese and USA exporters as well as the Union producers sold an additional AE product type does not detract from the Commission’s finding on cumulation of South African imports with other imports. Thus, the claim was rejected.

- (363) Moreover, and irrespective of the evolution of import volumes of the individual countries concerned, the price pattern followed by all countries concerned was the same. More specifically, the price followed a downward trend with the same price dynamics manifested over the period considered (prices peaking in 2022 to then sharply decline in 2023 and remain at low levels until the IP for all countries concerned). It was found to be equally immaterial whether the country concerned was considered a price setter or price taker on the market – important is that the imports from all countries concerned operated in the same market, were confronted with the same conditions and ultimately competed for the same customers.

#### 4.6.   **Economic situation of the Union industry**

##### 4.6.1.   *General remarks*

- (380) In accordance with Article 3(5) of the basic Regulation, the examination of the impact of the dumped imports on the Union industry included an evaluation of all economic indicators having a bearing on the state of the Union industry during the period considered.

- (381) As mentioned in recital (27), sampling was used for the determination of possible injury suffered by the Union industry.

- (382) For the injury determination, the Commission distinguished between macroeconomic and microeconomic injury indicators. The Commission evaluated the macroeconomic indicators on the basis of data contained in the questionnaire replies of the sampled Union producers and the reply to macro questionnaire submitted by Dow Europe. The data related to all Union producers. The Commission evaluated the microeconomic indicators on the basis of data contained in the questionnaire replies from the sampled Union producers. The data related to the sampled Union producers. Both sets of data were found to be representative of the economic situation of the Union industry.

###### 4.6.2.6.   Comments of the parties

- (403) Several parties, namely Pinghu Petro, Sasol, Gantrade, and Organik claimed that the Union industry did not suffer material injury under Article 3(5) of the basic Regulation, given that numerous key economic indicators (such as sales, market share, production) for the Union industry showed positive developments during the period considered. Pinghu Petro also noted in this context that the Commission has in its previous practice made a finding of non-injury where most indicators had experienced positive developments towards the end of the period considered.

- (404) First, the Commission noted that the assessment in each investigation is based on the facts and circumstances in that investigation. Moreover, the lawfulness of regulations imposing anti-dumping measures must be assessed in the light of legal rules and not based on the Commission’s alleged previous decisions.

- (405) Second, in line with Article 3(5) of the basic Regulation, the assessment of injury requires a comprehensive evaluation of all relevant economic factors and indices having a bearing on the state of the industry. The assessment involves a holistic appraisal of both macro- and microeconomic factors, while one or several of these factors in isolation do not necessarily give decisive guidance as to the outcome of the assessment. In this context, a finding of material injury does not require all relevant economic indicators to show a negative trend - which is the case here as set out in recital (428). Therefore, the claims raised by the parties had to be rejected.

#### 4.7.   **Conclusion on injury**

- (424) Although the absolute import quantity from the countries concerned fell by less than 12 kt in the period considered, the Union demand contracted even more sharply (-45 kt), so the relative importance of the imports remained high and the share of imports from the countries concerned stayed at 30 % in the IP (at the same levels as at the beginning of the period considered). Moreover, and importantly, the consistently low-priced dumped imports generated a severe price-depression in the Union market, with the gap between the cost of the Union producers and their revenue widening between 2023-IP. This price pressure, compounded by the consistently high volumes of imported AE in a contracting market, rendered the Union industry loss-making from 2023 onward.

- (425) The acrylic esters market operates with high fixed costs and requires a high capacity utilisation rate to achieve profitability. In particular, following the downward trend in energy prices from 2023, the Union industry should have been expected to maintain their production and sales levels stable despite a slightly contracting market thereby allowing the distribution of their fixed costs over a higher quantity produced and maintaining profitability. However, the prices of the dumped imports started plummeting following 2022, forcing the Union industry to lower their prices, while trying to keep a reasonable plant capacity utilization and control over their fixed costs. Whilst this approach allowed the Union industry to gain market share (mainly at the expense of imports from other third countries which were not found to be dumped), it completely eroded their profitability.

- (426) Macroeconomic indicators, such as Union production, production capacity, capacity utilisation remained broadly stable and employment rose modestly (7 % increase in head-count) over the period considered, while the Union producers managed to increase their sales volumes in the Union market as well as their market share. Moreover, the investment expenditure continued to rise. These positive trends illustrate the efforts of the Union AE industry to mitigate the adverse effects of the injurious dumping.

- (427) However, these developments did not translate into improved profitability because the Union industry was forced to lower its sales price far below cost to keep a relevant presence on the Union market in the face of sizeable, dumped imports. Following positive profitability, strong cash-flow and robust return on investment in 2022, from 2023 onwards the sector’s financial performance deteriorated sharply. Sales prices fell by 44 % over the period considered, while unit production costs fell much less (-26 %). Both cash flow and return on investment turned negative, and the net profitability of sales to unrelated customers collapsed from +12 % in 2022 to -13 % in the IP.

- (428) Overall, the sustained high import share of the countries concerned coupled with a pronounced price pressure amplified by market contraction created a price-depression environment that forced Union producers to sell below cost, generating losses. While indicators such as production, capacity utilisation, sales volumes, market share and investment showed limited positive movement, they were outweighed by the severe price and profitability erosion caused by the dumped imports. The combined effect of falling profitability, negative cash-flow, deteriorating returns on investment and reduced productivity will (if continued) severely limit the Union industry’s capacity to raise capital and to fund further investment, impeding any prospect of recovery or growth.

- (429) On the basis of the above, the Commission concluded at this stage that the Union industry suffered material injury within the meaning of Article 3(5) of the basic Regulation.

### 5. **CAUSATION**

- (430) In accordance with Article 3(6) of the basic Regulation, the Commission examined whether the dumped imports from the countries concerned caused material injury to the Union industry. In accordance with Article 3(7) of the basic Regulation, the Commission also examined whether other known factors could at the same time have injured the Union industry. The Commission ensured that any possible injury caused by factors other than the dumped imports from the countries concerned was not attributed to the dumped imports. These factors are imports from other third countries, export performance of the Union producers, effects of the raw material prices and the increased cost of production, decline in the Union consumption, alleged structural overcapacity and increase in investments.

#### 5.1.   **Effects of the dumped imports**

- (431) The import quantities from the countries concerned decreased during the period considered by 11,9 kt. However, this decrease in imports coincided with an almost four times higher drop in Union demand. Furthermore, the total market share of the imports from the countries concerned stayed consistently high and at or above 30% throughout the entire period considered.

- (432) In terms of price effects on the Union industry, the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic associated with an extraordinary surge in demand for AE-containing products helped the Union producers to rebuff injurious effects of the dumped imports in 2022 (beginning of the period considered). However, the price effects of the dumped imports became more pronounced from 2023 onwards, with the imports exerting price pressure on the Union industry in a shrinking market. While in 2022 the Union producers were profitable, the pressure from consistently high import volumes at dumped prices became unsustainable for the Union industry in the period that followed. From 2023 onwards, the Union industry became unable to keep its sales prices in line with the cost of production, despite the latter going down, to pass on to customers the cost of raw materials.

- (433) Dumped import prices effectively acted as the market benchmark, directly contributing to persistent losses despite continued production capability and presence of the Union producers in the Union market. Indeed, from being profitable in 2022, the Union industry became loss-making for the remainder of the period considered, with the situation looking dire in particular in the investigation period (-13 % loss).

- (434) Therefore, the low-priced imports from the countries concerned caused the prices of the Union industry to be depressed to a significant degree within the meaning of Article 3(3) of the basic Regulation.

- (435) In view of the above considerations, the Commission provisionally established that the material injury suffered by the Union industry was caused by the dumped imports from the countries concerned within the meaning of Article 3(6) of the basic Regulation. The consistently high level of imports from the countries concerned throughout the period considered was significant enough to cause material injury because of the low prices undercutting and depressing the prices of the Union industry.

- (436) Pinghu Petro argued that a China-specific causal link between the imports and the alleged injury as required under Article 3(6) of the basic Regulation was not established. Furthermore, Pinghu Petro submitted that there was a temporal disconnect between the evolution of import volumes and prices on one hand and the situation of the Union industry on the other hand. GAFT also contended that it is unlikely that import volumes from the countries concerned negatively affected the Union industry.

- (437) It was noted that conditions for cumulative assessment are met for the Chinese imports (see recitals (351)-(352)) and hence the Chinese imports were assessed together with imports from the other countries concerned and were not subject to individual assessment in this case, including for causality. In any event, the import statistics for China showed consistently high presence on the Union market over the period considered and a similar price trend to that observed for the other countries concerned (high prices in 2022, followed by a sharp decline in 2023, which was sustained until the end of the period considered).

- (438) With respect to the alleged lack of coincidence in time between imports and the deterioration of the state of Union industry, the following was noted. The existence of a causal link under Article 3(6) of the basic Regulation does not require a full temporal coincidence between the evolution of import prices/volumes and deterioration of Union industry’s situation. What is decisive is to establish whether the import volumes and prices and their evolution are responsible for an adverse, material impact on the Union industry.

- (439) In the present case, it was found that the dumped imports from the countries concerned, present in the Union market in consistently high volumes and at low price levels prevented the Union industry from reaching reasonable levels of profit throughout the period considered, in particular from 2023 onwards and hence caused injury to the Union industry. Pinghu Petro’s and GAFT’s arguments were therefore rejected.

- (448) Export volumes of the Union industry decreased over the period considered and mainly in 2023 and the IP, which is consistent with the defence strategy of the Union producers, prioritizing their presence in the Union market and their domestic sales accordingly. Furthermore, export prices followed the same trend as prices on the Union market over the period considered, which is attributable to the presence and similar price behaviour of the countries concerned also in the export markets of the Union producers such as Türkiye.

- (449) The Commission provisionally concluded that while the export performance of the Union industry contributed to the material injury, it did not attenuate the causal link between the dumped imports from the countries concerned and the injury found.

- (450) Several parties (Sasol, GAFT and Pinghu Petro) claimed that it was the decline in the export performance, characterised by a sharp decline in export volumes and a sustained price erosion across export markets that contributed to or can explain any alleged injury of the Union industry.

- (451) As set out in recital (435), while the export volumes of the Union producers declined over the period considered and the prices also decreased, this was due to the fact that imports from countries concerned were present and exhibited a similar price behaviour as in the Union also in these export markets.

- (452) In any event, it was recalled that imports from the countries concerned do not need to be the sole cause of that injury and that the existence of other contributing factors (such as export performance of the Union industry) does not negate the causal link, provided that the effects of imports remain distinguishable and significant (which is the case here).

#### 5.5.   **Decline in the Union consumption**

- (453) According to Gantrade, the post-2022 price and profitability decrease reflected a natural market correction and are not a result of injurious pricing from imports. Organik also claimed that despite the contraction in demand, Union producers managed to increase their production volumes, sales volumes and market share during the period concerned, which was difficult to reconcile with an allegation that imports caused material injury to the Union industry. Furthermore, Sasol claimed that the deterioration in Union sales prices, which, in turn, negatively affected profitability of the Union producers was not driven by imports from the countries concerned, but largely by the Union producers’ strategy to expand market share in a context of contracting demand. In the same vein, Pinghu Petro submitted that where injury flows from strategic pricing decisions in a deteriorating demand environment, it cannot be attributed to imports. Moreover, in Pinghu Petro’s view, the demand contraction can mechanically depress prices irrespective of imports and amplify competitive behaviour among suppliers seeking to defend volumes.

- (454) As outlined in recitals (348)-(349) above, indeed the Union consumption on the free market showed a decrease over the period considered. However, under normal conditions of competition, a demand contraction would not force economic operators with a market share exceeding 50 % to sell their products below cost at lossmaking levels (which was the case for Union producers) – certainly not over a prolonged period extending over three calendar years. Moreover, in 2024 when the Union consumption almost returned to 2022 levels (the year in which the Union producers enjoyed high profits), the profit levels of the Union producers remained in the negative.

- (455) In any event and contrary to what the parties argue, Union industry’s defensive behaviour which ultimately led to their increased sales volumes and market share and stable production levels, was not indicative of a healthy competition. Instead, it was indicative of a situation in which firms were forced to prioritise volume over profitability to remain present in the market and to confront the dumped imports.

- (456) In conclusion, the economic contraction contributed to the material injury of the Union industry, however not to the extent that it would attenuate the causal link between the injury suffered by the Union industry and the dumped imports from the countries concerned.

#### 5.6.   **Captive demand of the Union industry**

- (457) Gantrade argued that a contraction in demand, including from the Union industry’s own downstream operations, was a cause of material injury unrelated to the allegedly dumped imports.

- (458) Captive consumption followed a trend similar to the evolution of the free market consumption. In fact, as set out in recital (348), the degree of contraction of the captive demand was 50 % lower than the rate of the free market consumption decrease over the period considered. Moreover, the captive consumption even increased in the IP (when the material injury was most prominent) compared to 2024, which is not the case for the free market consumption. Hence the evolution of Union industry’s internal demand only had limited impact on the situation of the Union industry and at most contributed to the material injury, without breaking the causal link between the material injury suffered by the Union industry and the dumped imports.

#### 5.7.   **Cost of raw materials, energy and labour**

- (459) Gantrade argued that the challenges faced by the Union industry were attributable to structurally higher raw materials (propylene in this case) and energy costs for the Union industry, which inherently affected its competitiveness. Pinghu Petro also submitted that the Union industry was exposed to an exceptional energy-cost shock in 2022–2023, which sharply escalated production costs. Finally, according to Sasol, the evidence demonstrated that the sharp increase in inflation as well as the surge in energy costs in 2022-2023, accompanied by the rising labour costs throughout the period considered led to reduced margins and declining profitability for the Union producers.

- (460) Prices of propylene were indeed found to be elevated in 2022, while they stabilised at lower levels for the rest of the period considered. Furthermore, it was not disputed that the inflationary pressures and energy crisis in 2022-2023 affected the cost of production of the Union producers. However, the energy cost started decreasing and inflation decelerated and stabilised at lower levels from 2023 onwards. Furthermore, although average labour cost of the Union producers moderately increased over the period considered (+7 %), overall cost of production decreased by 26 % over the same period. Against this background, in 2022, despite the high raw material and energy prices and general inflationary pressures, the Union producers were able to increase their sales prices to absorb the high production cost and generate profits. On the other hand, following 2023 and up to the investigation period, when the upward trend in energy prices had reversed, propylene prices have stabilised and inflation has eased down, the profitability trend of the Union producers became negative to reach alarmingly negative levels in the investigation period. Hence while the Union producers succeeded in passing on the cost of raw material and energy prices in 2022, they failed to do so in the period that followed, even if the cost of production dropped considerably for the Union producers. Under conditions of fair competition, Union producers would have been able to pass their costs on to customers through higher sales prices. However, this ability was impeded by the presence of dumped imports, mainly from 2023 onwards.

- (461) In view of that, the temporary increases in cost of raw material and energy did not attenuate the causal link between the dumped imports originating in the countries concerned and the injury suffered by the Union industry.

#### 5.8.   **Structural overcapacity**

- (462) Sasol claimed that should material injury be established, this was caused largely by the significant overcapacities and inherent inefficiencies within the local production infrastructure at the Union producers.

- (463) As set out in recital (385) and contrary to Sasol’s claim, the operating rates at the Union producers were rather high over the period considered. Moreover, while in 2022 (when the Union industry still generated high profits) the Union industry operated at 71 % of the capacity, in the IP, the capacity utilisation was at a comparable 68 %, although the profitability of the Union industry was highly negative by then. Alleged overcapacity could therefore not have contributed to the material injury suffered by the Union industry.

#### 5.9.   **Pricing and investment decisions**

- (464) Pinghu Petro as well as Sasol submitted that the Union producers’ decision to invest heavily necessarily resulted in a downward pressure on profitability independently of imports. Sasol further claimed that a sustained and significant rise in investment was incompatible with a situation of material injury and instead demonstrates that the economic state of the Union industry remains financially robust.

- (465) As illustrated by a major decarbonization investment project undertaken by the Union industry, this type of investments is a result of strategic decisions taken as part of a multiannual framework and requires years of preparation. Moreover, these investment decisions are taken at the enterprise level (rather than at the level of a product department), and are part of wider, global corporate initiatives [^ntr162-L_202602512EN.000101-E0162]. Therefore, and as correctly pointed out also by Sasol in their submission, such investments reflect long-term strategic objectives. They do not occur in response to short-term market fluctuations, are forward-looking, not necessarily product-specific and hence cannot explain a sudden deterioration in profitability and resulting injury in this case and for AE specifically. By the same token, the fact that a producer undertakes significant investments which will likely result in a positive effect in a mid- to long-term does not preclude a finding of material injury caused by dumped imports.

- (466) Pinghu Petro also argued that the operation of outdated production facilities in the Union typically entails significantly higher costs and inferior production efficiencies and may constitute an autonomous source of injury.

- (467) Pinghu Petro’s claim was considered speculative and unsubstantiated. As set out in recital (419) and contrary to the claim, the Union industry made continuous investments into their production facilities going beyond maintenance and regulatory compliance. These investments improve energy efficiency of the facilities and at the same time ensure that the Union producers maintain a relevant presence in the Union market. Therefore, Pinghu Petro’s claim was dismissed.

#### 5.10.   **Conclusion on causation**

- (468) A causal link was established between the dumped imports from the countries concerned on one hand and the injury suffered by the Union industry on the other hand. In particular after 2022, the dumped imports remained high (around 30-35 % of total free Union consumption) and their prices fell markedly. In a relatively weak market with demand contraction, the increasingly low-priced and sizeable dumped imports eroded margins of the Union industry and its ability to set prices that would absorb the high costs of production, thereby clearly pointing to the existence of price depression. This situation resulted in a lossmaking situation of the Union producers in 2023-IP.

- (469) The Commission examined other possible factors that may have had an impact on the situation of the Union industry. The effects of these factors on the situation of the Union industry were distinguished and separated from the injurious effects of the dumped imports.

- (470) Regarding the effects of imports from other third countries, the Commission concluded that these imports fell in both volume and market share (from [19–23] % to [6–10] %) and their average prices stayed both above Union sales prices and import prices from the countries concerned, so they did not contribute to the material injury suffered by the Union producers.

- (471) The export price performance of the Union industry was affected by a similar pattern to that observed for the Union sales over the period considered, while the export quantities dropped by 19 % contrary to domestic sales which rose in the pursuit of volume defence strategy. While the effect of the export performance of the Union producers contributed to the adverse situation at the Union producers, it did not break the causal link between the dumped imports and the injury suffered by the Union industry.

- (472) Regarding the contraction of Union consumption (both on free market as well as captive consumption), it amplified the price pressure and contributed to the material injury; however, it was not liable to break the causal link established between the material injury suffered and dumped imports. Moreover, although the raw material and energy costs rose in 2022, they subsequently fell, yet the Union industry became unprofitable from 2023 onward, indicating that cost evolution cannot explain the injury. Lastly, the material injury cannot be explained by any alleged overcapacities (that were moreover not established), nor by long-term investments made at the enterprise level in a strategic move to enhance competitiveness of the Union industry in the chemical sector.

- (473) On the basis of the above, the Commission concluded at this stage that the dumped imports from the countries concerned caused material injury to the Union industry (notably in 2023-IP) and that the other factors, considered individually or collectively, did not attenuate the causal link between the dumped imports and the material injury. The injury consists principally of severe price depression, loss of profitability, negative cash flow and negative return on investments.

- (474) Pinghu Petro submitted that the potential causality factors (other than dumped imports) must be assessed cumulatively in this case, as they arise concurrently and interact in a mutually reinforcing manner amplifying the combined impact. Sasol made a similar claim, arguing that a combination of other factors breaks the causal link.

- (475) Contrary to the arguments of the parties, while the factors other than dumped imports exacerbated individually or collectively the injurious situation of the Union industry, there was a clear and identifiable causal link between the material injury suffered and the dumped imports from the countries concerned, which is not attenuated by any of these other factors. The arguments raised by the parties must therefore be rejected.

### 6. **LEVEL OF MEASURES**

- (476) The complainants claimed the existence of raw material distortions within the meaning of Article 7(2a) of the basic Regulation in the complaint.

- (477) Thus, in order to conduct the assessment on the appropriate level of measures, the Commission first established the amount of duty necessary to eliminate the injury suffered by the Union industry in the absence of distortions under Article 7(2a) of the basic Regulation.

- (478) Then it examined whether the dumping margin of sampled exporting producers would be higher than their injury margin.

#### 6.1.   **Injury margin**

- (479) The injury would be removed if the Union industry were able to obtain a target profit by selling at a target price in the sense of Articles 7(2c) and 7(2d) of the basic Regulation.

- (480) In accordance with Article 7(2c) of the basic Regulation, for establishing the target profit, the Commission took into account the following factors: the level of profitability before the increase of imports from the countries concerned, the level of profitability needed to cover full costs and investments, research and development (R & D) and innovation, and the level of profitability to be expected under normal conditions of competition. Such profit margin should not be lower than 6 %.

- (481) The profit for the sampled Union producers widely fluctuated within the period considered. The exceptionally high profits in 2022 were achieved in an extraordinary period of demand fuelled by the COVID-19 economy and supply chain restraints, as also acknowledged by the complainant. Therefore, it would not be appropriate to use the profit figures from 2022 as a basic profit in accordance with Article 7(2c) of the basic Regulation. Furthermore, for the years prior to the period considered, the Union producers could not reliably identify precise levels of profitability. The Commission therefore established the target profit to determine the non-injurious price at 6 %, in accordance with Article 7(2c) of the basic Regulation.

- (482) Furthermore, none of the sampled Union producers made a substantiated claim for investments foregone or R & D and innovation costs. In view of those facts, the Commission resorted to the use of the minimum 6 % target profit which was added to the Union industry’s actual cost of production to establish the non-injurious price.

- (483) As no substantiated claims were made pursuant to Article 7(2d) concerning current or future costs which result from multilateral environmental agreements and protocols thereunder or from the listed ILO Conventions, no further costs were added to the non-injurious price thus established.

- (484) The Commission then determined the injury margin level on the basis of a comparison of the weighted average import price of the sampled cooperating exporting producers in the countries concerned as established for the price undercutting calculations, with the weighted average non-injurious price of the like product sold by the sampled Union producers on the Union market during the investigation period. Any difference resulting from this comparison was expressed as a percentage of the weighted average import CIF value.

- (485) Due to non-cooperation from the sole Saudi exporting producer, the injury margin for Saudi Arabia was based on facts available. The Saudi export price was compared to the highest individual target price of the like product produced by the sampled Union producers.

- (486) The injury elimination level for ‘other cooperating companies’ and for ‘all other imports’ originating in the respective country concerned is defined in the same manner as the dumping margin for these companies and imports.

#### 6.2.   **Examination of the margin adequate to remove the injury to the Union industry**

- (487) As explained in the Notice of Initiation, the complainant provided the Commission sufficient evidence that there are raw material distortions in China regarding the product under investigation. Therefore, in accordance with Article 7(2a) of the basic Regulation, this investigation examined the alleged distortions to assess whether, if relevant, a duty lower than the margin of dumping would be sufficient to remove injury.

- (488) However, as the margins adequate to remove injury are higher than the dumping margins with regard to the Chinese exporting producers, the Commission considered that at this stage it was not necessary to address this aspect.

### 7. **UNION INTEREST**

- (490) Having decided to apply Article 7(2) of the basic Regulation, the Commission examined whether it could clearly conclude that it was not in the Union interest to adopt measures in this case, despite the determination of injurious dumping, in accordance with Article 21 of the basic Regulation.

- (491) The determination of the Union interest was based on an appreciation of all the various interests involved, including those of the Union industry, importers and users.

#### 7.1.   **Interest of the Union industry**

- (492) The Union industry for AE consists of four producers that manufactured the product during the investigation period. This industry is an important part of the EU industrial base, as acrylate monomers are used in a wide range of downstream products, including coatings, adhesives, sealants, paper, polymer additives, textiles and inks.

- (493) The imposition of anti-dumping measures would likely help Union producers restore profitability and stabilise cash flow. By improving profitability, Union producers would be better placed to maintain compliance with environmental rules, invest in research, development and lower-carbon production and operational efficiency. Furthermore, the imposition of measures would enable the Union industry to preserve employment and security of supply within the Union. A preservation of the Union production at sustainable levels would also support upstream petrochemical industries, in particular through higher and more stable demand for propylene and related feedstocks.

- (494) By contrast, without corrective measures, the Union industry would face further pressure from unfairly low-priced imports, leading to deeper losses and threatening a long-term viability of the Union production. Continued price depression would make it difficult to sustain operations and preserve jobs. In such a situation, further investment would likely be limited, while plans for long-term competitiveness could be delayed or abandoned altogether.

- (495) In addition, the continued deterioration of the AE business would affect the downstream activities at the Union producers, which are operationally or economically linked to the production of the product under investigation. The downsizing or loss of upstream AE production in the Union would therefore have wider implications for the sustainability of related value-chain activities within the Union.

- (496) The Commission therefore concluded that imposing anti-dumping duties would be in the interest of the Union industry.

#### 7.2.   **Interest of unrelated importers**

- (497) With respect to unrelated importers, two companies, Opes International Limited (‘Opes’) and Gantrade, cooperated in this investigation, including by providing replies to the Commission questionnaire. Gantrade was opposed to the imposition of the measures in this case, while Opes did not publicly express its position on the measures.

- (498) It is provisionally considered that the measures will likely have an insignificant impact (if any) on importers as they can continue importing AE at fair prices from the countries concerned or other third countries, creating a healthy competition for the domestically produced AE. Furthermore, the evidence provided by the cooperating unrelated importers shows that more than half of their imports in value concerns products other than AE, hence further diluting the effect of measures on these importers and demonstrating that the importers can diversify their business activities to other products.

#### 7.3.   **Interest of users**

- (499) In the present case, users predominantly representing the acrylic resins segment came forward, opposing the measures The resins are an intermediary product used for the main user applications of the product concerned – coatings adhesives and additives. Several users (Allnex Belgium SA / NV (‘Allnex’), Covestro AG, Organik, EPS B.V., ICAP-SIRA Chemicals and Polymers S.p.A., Trinseo PLC and Dow [^ntr163-L_202602512EN.000101-E0163]) replied to the user questionnaire made available at the beginning of the investigation, while other users (Bear Quimica, s.a. (‘Bear’), Alberdingk Boley GmbH (‘Alberdingk’) and Follmann Chemie GmbH (‘Follmann’)) solely submitted their written position on the investigation.

- (500) For the users that completed the dedicated questionnaire, their financial performance showed that all (save for one user) enjoyed healthy profit margins, ranging between 3–36 % in the investigation period. and one user was lossmaking, close to breakeven – however, the proportion of the turnover generated on the AE-inclusive product for the user concerned accounted for less than 10 % of its total company turnover.

- (501) Moreover, the submissions by majority of users indicated that the proportion of the AE cost on their cost of production was limited and accounted (for five out of seven users) for no more than 3–15 % of the cost of production. Regarding the two most affected users which have the highest share of cost of the product under investigation in their total cost of production (20–30 %), they were profitable (with profits around 3–5 % and 16–20 % respectively). Moreover, both companies purchased the vast majority of AE (60–85 %) from the Union and therefore the proposed duties would have a limited effect on their profitability. Considering the above, the measures are likely to have a limited and manageable effect on the users.

- (502) The measures are also not expected to cause shortages for the users. Despite the imposition of measures, users would still be able to import from the countries concerned, since anti-dumping duties are intended to correct unfairly low prices, not to block imports completely. In addition, commercially relevant quantities have been imported into the Union from third countries not subject to measures (such as South Korea, Japan and Taiwan) during the period considered. Hence imports from these countries can provide users with alternative sources of acrylic esters. Union producers also have spare capacity and could increase output without needing new investment in enlarging capacity.

#### 7.4.   **Comments of the interested parties**

- (503) The Chinese exporting producer Pinghu Petro claimed that the imposition of anti-dumping duties against imports of AE from China would drive up costs for Union downstream users in a wide variety of sectors. Sasol made a similar claim, adding that higher production costs across the downstream value chain would ultimately be borne by Union consumers. Organik, Allnex, Gantrade, Bear, Alberdingk and Follmann also argued that imposition of duties would result in cost increases for the Union downstream operators (with limited ability to absorb such increases or pass them on) and compression of their margins, which would potentially lead to their reduced competitiveness and to increased imports of downstream products (since measures would not apply to the downstream products). According to parties, this situation could ultimately lead to relocation of the downstream industries outside the Union.

- (504) While the anti-dumping measures may result in some increase in input prices for users, such increase is expected to be limited and manageable. Having regard to the current profitability record of the users relative to the profitability (or rather lack thereof) of the Union producers, downstream users can be expected to pass on any cost increases along the value chain. Moreover, the price effects of the measures can be mitigated by contractual pricing arrangements and diversified sourcing practices, which are already implemented by the users. Continued presence of imports from countries concerned (also in view of the level of duties imposed) and other third countries on the Union market in addition to the four Union producers will also ensure the competitive pressure which is likely to mitigate any potential cost increases that might arise from the imposition of these duties. In any event, it is observed that the measures will primarily correct artificially low import prices (at which the users were buying the material for an extended period) that currently undercut Union producers. Having regard to the above, the imposition of measures should not have a prohibitive effect on the users and their competitiveness let alone force them to relocate outside the Union. The arguments of the parties were therefore rejected.

- (505) Moreover, it is reasonable to consider that the impact on the Union consumers will be immaterial, given the overall cost structure of the final consumer products in this segment (such as acrylic paints purchased in a DIY store) and the fact that these final products are more resilient to fluctuations in prices of the intermediates that enter the value chain much higher upstream.

- (506) In conclusion, hypothetical adverse effects on the user industries and on final consumers cannot outweigh the distinct need to restore a level playing field on the Union AE market, in particular where the long-term viability of the Union industry and sustainability of related value-chain activities within the Union are at stake. The alternative scenario without duties and with the Union industry disappearing would be more harmful for the users than a price increase the imposition of duties might lead to.

- (507) Organik argued that anti-dumping measures would restrict imports from countries concerned, which consequently would further exacerbate the AE supply constraints for independent downstream users, given that the AE Union market is highly concentrated and a substantial share of AE is used captively. Gantrade also argued that imposition of duties would significantly reduce the availability of AE in the Union market. Furthermore, according to Pinghu Petro, the likely consequence of anti-dumping duties would be to effectively foreclose the Union market altogether for Chinese imports. Also in the South African exporting producer Sasol’s view, the competitiveness of Union downstream manufacturers would be undermined by potentially limiting access to AE if duties significantly reduce import volumes. Bear, Alberdingk and Follmann also claimed that vertically integrated Union producers could prioritize captive supplies to merchant market sales after imposition of measures.

- (508) First, the Commission recalled that the Union AE market is competitive, covering four Union producers as well as other exporting countries such as Korea, or Taiwan in addition to the countries concerned (including China), which will not be prevented from entering the Union at fair and non-injurious prices. Imposition of anti-dumping duties is hence not prone to lead to an increased concentration of Union producers in the AE market. On the other hand, and contrary to parties’ claims, imposition of measures will ensure continued presence of the Union-based suppliers and hence reliable supply base for Union users, especially in the current geopolitical context, where certain events (such as closure of key shipping gateways) can result in unavailability of imports. Moreover, the parties did not provide any substantiated evidence that the Union producers and other existing sources of supply would not be able to satisfy the demand on the Union market.

- (509) Second, the level of captive consumption has been stable over the period considered and any claim that this trend is bound to change and that supply constraints would increase for independent users is speculative and unfounded. As outlined in recital (393), free market sales by the Union industry accounted for around 45 % of their combined total Union sales and usage and that proportion even increased to 47–48 % in 2024-IP. Furthermore, the Union industry has been operating at around 70 % of the installed capacity during the period considered and hence there is a spare capacity in the Union to satisfy any potential increase in demand from independent downstream users.

- (510) Organik and Gantrade further submitted that suppliers located in alternative third markets such as Korea, Japan or Taiwan cannot offer sufficient and scalable volumes capable of replacing the subject imports on a sustained basis. Contrary to the claim of the parties and as it follows from recitals (440)-(441), AE was imported into the Union in commercially significant quantities from South Korea, Taiwan and Japan, with their combined market share of 6,5 % in the IP showing relevant and non-negligible presence in the Union market. Earlier in the period considered, sales volumes from these countries were even higher. Moreover, users such as Organik do not need to rely exclusively on alternative sources of supply, especially since they traditionally sought to source most of their products from Union producers. In addition, the parties can obviously continue to import the material from countries concerned, at reasonable and non-injurious prices. Organik’s and Gantrade’s arguments were therefore rejected as unfounded.

- (511) Organik also stated that for the company, product type BA is a core input representing a significant component of Organik’s cost structure.

- (512) While Organik might be more exposed to an increase in AE cost relative to some other downstream users, the impact on this user cannot outweigh the interest in fair trade restoration and the need to prevent further deterioration of the Union industry for numerous reasons. First, Organik already applies diversified sourcing strategy, sourcing the material from combination of suppliers and notably 50-70 % from the Union producers. As outlined by Organik in their own submission, where downstream users source inputs from a mix of domestic and imported supplies and are not highly dependent on the imports from countries concerned (which is the case here), the cost impact of duties may be mitigated. Second, contrary to Organik’s claim, as set out in recital (407), the sales price in the Union was consistently decreasing during the period considered and the users (including Organik) actually benefitted for an extended period from artificially low prices. Hence, any price correction would merely be a manifestation of the fair-trade restoration in a situation where Union industry prices were depressed by a downward pressure coming from the dumped imports. In any event, imposition of duties and any resulting price increase for AE should not prevent downstream users from increasing prices to pass on any such cost increase. Organik did not provide any evidence to the contrary.

- (513) Furthermore, Organik raised a concern that existing structural asymmetries between vertically integrated Union producers and their downstream operations on one hand and non-integrated downstream operators (without captive access to AE) on the other hand would be further exacerbated if duties are imposed. Gantrade also submitted that several Union producers are vertically integrated, operating their own downstream activities and are able to source key raw materials internally. According to Gantrade, they would effectively shield their downstream operations from the full impact of price increases caused by the proposed duties. The exporting producer Sasol, and the other users Bear, Alberdingk and Follmann made a similar claim, submitting that raw material costs would increase for non-integrated downstream users relative to vertically integrated acrylic-monomer producers.

- (514) It was not disputed that there is an asymmetry between the vertically integrated and non-integrated operators. However, anti-dumping duties serve a clear regulatory purpose of restoring fair trade, should legitimate interest on the part of the Union industry be established and conditions for imposition of measures be met (which is the case here). Trade defence measures operate independently and irrespective of any market structures in place and do not have the objective of curing any existing or potential market imperfections. Moreover, the parties seem to ignore in their arguments the fact that unlike independent downstream users, the vertically integrated Union producers face considerable capital expenditures and carry significant business risks associated with the production of the upstream material (in this case AE) used then in their downstream operations, including fluctuations in costs of raw material and energy, irrespective of any impact that the duties may have. The claims of the parties were therefore dismissed.

- (515) The exporting producer – Dow – stated that unfairly traded imports of AE from China already adversely affect Dow’s downstream businesses in the Union and that imposing duties on imports from the US would further undermine Dow’s ability to compete in downstream markets in the Union and in export markets.

- (516) First, measures introduced on the Chinese imports of AE with a view to restore fair trade in the Union are addressing Dow’s concerns on imports originating from China. Second, regarding the duties imposed on the US imports, Dow can source (at least BA) internally also from Dow Union operations independently of the Dow US imports. Moreover, Dow will still be able to source AE from the US, at reasonable and non-injurious prices.

- (517) In Pinghu Petro’s view, imposition of anti-dumping duties would not be in the Union interest, as the wider Union industry faces an energy shock caused by the Middle East crisis, which should be addressed by a Union-wide industrial policy measure, rather than by anti-dumping measures. Pinghu Petro failed to substantiate the link between the factor alleged and the Union interest analysis in this case and its claim was therefore rejected.

- (518) Finally, the Hellenic Association of Chemical Industries (Hellenic association’) acting on behalf of Greek downstream users, expressed an opposition against measures in this case. It was noted that the Hellenic association came forward with their submission late in the procedure, almost six months into the investigation. For the sake of completeness, the association raised arguments made also by other interested parties, such as cost pressure on downstream industries and on final consumers as a result of the measures, security of supply concern, possible arbitrage on the imported downstream goods incorporating AE unencumbered by the duties on AE, loss of (export) competitiveness of the downstream users, structural asymmetry between the independent, non-integrated downstream users and vertically integrated producers. Conclusions set out in recitals (504)-(514) apply accordingly to address these arguments raised by the Hellenic association.

- (519) In addition, the Hellenic association submitted that part of the downstream industry (waterborne resins) will be subject to cumulative burden of AE duties, together with duties imposed on imports of Chinese titanium dioxide (also used by the producers of resins) in January 2025 [^ntr164-L_202602512EN.000101-E0164]. Contrary to what the association claimed and as it follows from the user submissions and their profitability record in 2025, their economic performance does not seem to have been adversely impacted by the titanium dioxide measures. The same preliminary conclusion can also be drawn with respect to the AE measures introduced, for the reasons set out in recital (500) .

- (520) The Hellenic association equally requested (i) an end-use relief in a form of end-use exemption, duty free tariff rate quota or a suspension of duties, while referring to the previous Commission practice or (ii) an inward processing relief. First, the reference to the precedents in the Commission’s practice is ineffective, since. the lawfulness of anti-dumping regulations must be assessed in the light of the basic Regulation and not based on the Commission’s previous decisions. Second, in the current case, the investigation concerns highly commoditized products (BA and 2EHA) rather than any specialty products to even consider an end-use exemption. Furthermore, no exceptional circumstances or supply constraints have been identified to warrant any duty-free tariff-rate quota or duty suspension. Regarding the inward processing, anti-dumping duties are only imposed on dumped products that are released for free circulation within the Union. Thus, anti-dumping duties do not apply to products brought into the customs territory of the Union under special customs procedures that does not involve entry for free circulation, such as inward processing,

#### 7.5.   **Conclusion on Union interest**

- (521) When weighing the effects, the positive consequences of imposing measures for the Union industry—namely restoring fair competition, preserving production and employment, and strengthening supply security—outweigh the limited negative consequences for users and importers. Given the largely commoditised nature of the product and the wide range of alternative sources of supply as well as overall positive economic performance of users, measures are not expected to lead to disproportionate cost increases or shortages. Moreover, the impact on users will likely be short-term as opposed to possibly irreversible and long-term effect that absence of measures would have on the operations of the Union producers.

- (522) On the basis of the above, the Commission concluded that there were no compelling reasons that it was not in the Union interest to impose measures on imports of AE originating in countries concerned at this stage of the investigation.
